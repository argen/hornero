# Tanya Reilly — distilled

> *The Staff Engineer's Path: A Guide for Individual Contributors Navigating Growth and Change* (2022, O'Reilly). The most complete single-volume treatment of the staff-IC role; accessible to engineers who have not yet reached that level.

## Why it matters for AI-coding

As AI handles more implementation, the comparative advantage of experienced engineers shifts toward design, coordination, and written communication. Reilly's book describes exactly these activities: how to write a design document that enables scrutiny, how to lead without authority, how to do the "glue work" that keeps a project coherent — none of which AI accelerates in any direct way. Staff engineers in AI-augmented teams will spend a higher fraction of their time on Reilly's crafts, not a lower one.

## Key concepts the role pulls from

- **Design-doc craft.** Reilly's treatment of how to write a design document — audience-explicit, right level of detail, narrative arc — is the closest practitioner guide to what the methodology's `rfc` skill produces. Her "tell a story" framing is a useful corrective to RFCs that read as a sequence of facts.
- **Staff-without-authority.** The core operating constraint: staff ICs drive decisions, shape design, and influence teams without a reporting relationship. The skill is persuasion-through-writing and building technical credibility over time, not positional authority.
- **Glue work.** The untracked work that holds a project together — clarifying ambiguous requirements, documenting decisions, chasing down cross-team dependencies, onboarding new engineers. Reilly names it, argues it's undervalued, and provides a framework for making it legible and appropriately credited.
- **Three maps.** Reilly's framework for navigating an organisation: the locator map (where you sit), the topographic map (what the terrain is — who has influence, what projects matter), and the treasure map (where you're going). A diagnostic tool for staff engineers who feel stuck.
- **Finite bandwidth on purpose.** Reilly's argument that deliberately scoping down is a staff skill, not a failure mode. Choosing what to work on is load-bearing; the engineer who works on everything influential does nothing influentially.
- **Project management as staff work.** Large technical projects without a PM need someone playing the coordination role; Reilly describes the basic project-management skills (milestone maps, risk registers, stakeholder communication) a staff engineer must pick up, reluctantly or not.

## What to actually read (1-hour budget)

- **Ch. 1 — "What Does a Staff Engineer Actually Do?"** — grounds the archetypes and the authority-without-hierarchy reality.
- **Ch. 4 — "Finite Time"** — the prioritisation model; the argument for scoping down deliberately.
- **Ch. 5 — "Leading Big Projects"** — the project-management-as-staff-skill argument; the design-doc section lives here.
- **Ch. 7 — "You're a Role Model Now (Sorry)"** — the glue-work argument; the most cited chapter by engineers who have experienced the cost of unacknowledged coordination work.

## What to skip

- Ch. 2-3 on organisational navigation ("Three maps") — useful for career development; peripheral for the methodology's skill invocations. Return to them when an engineer is troubleshooting a stuck influence problem.
- Ch. 8-9 on culture and team health — valuable but not load-bearing for the role's design and decision skills.

## How this anchors role skills

- [`rfc`](../../roles/software-engineer/skills/rfc/SKILL.md) — Reilly's design-doc craft (audience-explicit, narrative arc, right level of detail) is cited in the RFC skill's Key Concepts. Her "straw-man alternative is worse than no alternative" rule appears in the Pitfalls section directly.
- General staff-engineer mindset: the role invokes Reilly when coaching on writing as influence — the design doc that avoids a meeting, the clear RFC that gets async sign-off without a sync escalation.

## Cross-role connection

Reilly's glue-work argument maps onto the cross-functional-orchestration skill and the PM role's coordination surface. In orgs where no PM is present, a staff engineer typically absorbs the glue-work that a PM would own — requirements clarification, stakeholder updates, decision logging. Reilly makes the cost of that absorption legible; Torres and Cagan describe the canonical PM work that removes the need for engineers to absorb it.
