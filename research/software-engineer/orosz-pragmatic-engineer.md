# Gergely Orosz — distilled

> *The Pragmatic Engineer* newsletter (substack.com/p/pragmatic-engineer, 2020–present) and *The Software Engineer's Guidebook* (2023, self-published). Orosz writes data-rich essays on the engineering-org reality at Big Tech and high-growth companies, grounded in surveys, interviews, and his own Uber/Skype experience.

## Why it matters for AI-coding

Orosz is the empirical-practitioner counterpoint to the books on this list: while Brooks and Evans write from first principles, Orosz reports what is actually happening inside engineering orgs right now, including how teams are adapting (and failing to adapt) to AI-assisted development. His AI-coding coverage surveys what's shipping in production, what productivity claims hold up under scrutiny, and where the failure modes are concentrating — precisely the ground-truth the methodology needs to calibrate its AI-era claims against practice.

## Key concepts the role pulls from

- **Working-engineer canon.** Orosz's newsletter defines an implicit standard for what "above the bar" engineering looks like at strong companies: technical depth, cross-functional collaboration, written communication, and ownership of outcomes, not just tasks. The methodology borrows this tacit standard as a reference point for role expectations.
- **Inside-engineering-org transparency.** Orosz's distinguishing move is to open the black box of how tech companies actually operate — compensation bands, leveling rubrics, oncall practices, performance review mechanics. Useful when the methodology needs to calibrate advice against real-org constraints rather than idealised ones.
- **Calibrated AI-productivity reporting.** Orosz consistently applies empirical discipline to AI-productivity claims: which teams are seeing real gains, where gains are concentrated (greenfield vs maintenance, junior vs senior), what the adoption ceiling looks like, and what organisations are discovering about AI-generated code quality in production. The methodology cites this body of work when it needs to ground AI-era claims in data rather than hype.
- **Promotion and leveling as engineering-org literacy.** *The Software Engineer's Guidebook* maps the skills and artifacts that correlate with career progression across companies. Relevant to the methodology because it makes the implicit explicit: what does "staff-level impact" look like in practice, not just in principle.
- **The "boring" engineering argument.** Orosz consistently surfaces data showing that reliable, unsexy engineering — good oncall hygiene, clear runbooks, boring technology choices, incremental improvement — outperforms heroic innovation at the org-health level. The methodology's reliability canon (Google SRE) and Orosz's reporting converge here.

## What to actually read (1-hour budget)

- **Newsletter archive** — search for Orosz's AI-coding coverage (2023-present); the surveys of AI tool adoption and the "what's actually shipping" reporting are the most time-sensitive material.
- *The Software Engineer's Guidebook*, **Part I — "The Trajectory"** and **Part III — "On Software Engineering"** — the working-engineer standards section; the clearest single-volume treatment of what above-bar engineering looks like by level.
- Orosz's essay "The kind of engineering I prefer" (freely available) — the "boring engineering" argument in one sitting.

## What to skip

- *The Software Engineer's Guidebook*'s career-navigation chapters (salary negotiation, job searching) — valuable for the individual; peripheral to the methodology's skill invocations.
- Newsletter archives pre-2022 — focus on the more recent AI-era material unless a historical data point is specifically needed.

## How this anchors role skills

- General calibration: Orosz is the role's empirical ground-truth source. When a skill or review heuristic makes a claim about what "real" engineering orgs do, Orosz's reporting is the citation that either supports or qualifies it.
- [`rfc`](../../roles/software-engineer/skills/rfc/SKILL.md) and [`adr`](../../roles/software-engineer/skills/adr/SKILL.md) — Orosz's reporting on which documentation practices are actually adopted (vs which are theory) informs the "what to actually read" and "what to skip" calibration across the engineer role's skills.

## Cross-role connection

Orosz is the engineer-role's parallel to the tester role's Elisabeth Hendrickson — both are empirical-practitioner reporters who ground their domain in what works in real orgs, not what works in theory. Both write from direct observation, use data to qualify canonical claims, and focus on the gap between what the books say and what teams actually ship. Reading Orosz and Hendrickson together gives the methodology its empirical floor beneath the canonical bibliography.
