# Rory Sutherland — distilled

> *Alchemy: The Surprising Power of Ideas That Don't Make Sense* (Penguin, 2019). Behavioural economics applied to advertising and marketing — the case that purchase decisions are partly irrational and that framing matters more than facts.

## Why it matters for AI-coding

AI marketing tools optimise for rational claims and quantitative pitch: better ROI, lower cost, faster time-to-value. Sutherland's central argument is that this optimisation is built on a flawed model of human decision-making. Most purchase decisions are influenced by framing, context, and psycho-logic — factors that a rational-claim optimiser will systematically underweight or miss. The `strategic-narrative` skill's promised-land construction and the `launch-brief`'s framing checks are the procedural correctives; Sutherland provides the theoretical basis for why rational-only pitch fails and what the alternatives look like.

## Key concepts the role pulls from

- **Psycho-logic vs. logic.** Sutherland's central term: the logic of human perception and decision-making, which differs systematically from economic rationality. Adding lemon to bleach makes it sell better — not because lemon makes bleach work better, but because the smell changes the perception of effectiveness. The marketing-expert reviewer cites psycho-logic when a launch plan assumes rational decision-making as the only lever.
- **The irrational but reliable.** Sutherland documents a long list of cases where interventions that make no rational sense reliably change behaviour — the Eurostar example (adding a bar car increased perceived journey value more than cutting 40 minutes), the Uber waiting-time display (knowing the wait is 6 minutes is better than waiting 3 minutes without information). These are not flukes; they are how human perception works. Grounds the `launch-brief`'s framing-over-facts heuristic.
- **Framing is the product.** The price of a bottle of wine is a signal about its quality — a real product attribute, not a distortion. Reframing the context of a product often creates more value than improving the product itself. The marketing-expert reviewer asks "are we optimising the product or the frame?" when launch plans default to feature improvement as the only growth lever.
- **The bias toward logical solutions.** Sutherland's diagnostic of the consulting and engineering mind: they default to rational, measurable interventions and resist interventions that "make no sense." This bias causes systematically suboptimal decisions in marketing — and in AI marketing tools that are trained to produce rational arguments. The `pre-launch-marketing-audit`'s psycho-logic check is the corrective.
- **Opposite also works.** One of Sutherland's heuristics: if the rational approach seems obvious, ask whether the opposite might also work — and if so, why? This heuristic surfaces counterintuitive opportunities that data-first optimisation misses. The marketing-expert reviewer uses it to stress-test launch plans that have converged on the obvious.

## What to actually read (1-hour budget)

- *Alchemy* — Chapters 1, 2, 4, and 9: the behavioural-economics foundations, the Eurostar and Uber examples, the obliquity chapter, and the final chapter on alchemy as a practice. These carry Sutherland's load-bearing claims.
- "Rory Sutherland: Perspective is Everything" — his TED talk (~17 minutes). The best single-sitting introduction to psycho-logic; easier to internalize than the book for first read.

## What to skip

- Several of *Alchemy*'s middle chapters (5-8) are extended riffs on specific case studies that illustrate the same central mechanism. Read one; you'll have the principle. Skip the rest unless you're building a stakeholder presentation and need the anecdote library.
- Sutherland's Twitter/X presence — valuable for specific provocations but not structured learning. The book is the canonical form.

## How this anchors role skills

- [`strategic-narrative`](../../roles/marketing-expert/skills/strategic-narrative/SKILL.md) — the promised-land and winners/losers beats benefit from a Sutherland frame: are the stakes rational (cost, time) or also perceptual and emotional? The best narratives engage both registers. Sutherland justifies the emotional and perceptual dimension against "just show the ROI" objections.
- [`launch-brief`](../../roles/marketing-expert/skills/launch-brief/SKILL.md) — the brief's framing review is a Sutherland pass: does the launch plan over-index on rational claims and underweight framing, context, and perception?
- [`pre-launch-marketing-audit`](../../roles/marketing-expert/skills/pre-launch-marketing-audit/SKILL.md) — the audit's psycho-logic check flags launch plans that assume rational purchase decisions as the only mechanism. Sutherland is the evidentiary basis for flagging that assumption.
- [`positioning-canvas`](../../roles/marketing-expert/skills/positioning-canvas/SKILL.md) — Sutherland's framing-is-the-product insight reinforces that the canvas's market-category choice is itself a framing decision — choosing a category sets the perceptual context before the rational attributes are evaluated.

## Cross-role connection

Sutherland's behavioural framing connects directly to the designer's domain: both Sutherland and the designer role are concerned with the gap between rational intent and how decisions actually get made. The designer asks "what does the user perceive and feel?" before asking "what do they rationally evaluate?" — this is Sutherland's psycho-logic operationalised at the interface level. When the designer-reviewer and the marketing-expert-reviewer both flag a launch that assumes rational evaluation, Sutherland is the shared ancestor of both critiques. Pair this note with `research/designer/` entries on affordances and Kahneman's System 1/System 2 framing when the team is working at the intersection of product design and launch messaging.
