# David Ogilvy — distilled

> *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983) and *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963). The canonical advertising craft canon: testing-driven copy, the long-copy thesis, and the discipline of writing for the reader.

## Why it matters for AI-coding

AI-generated copy is technically competent and reliably forgettable. It averages the training corpus — producing sentences that no editor would flag but no reader would remember. Ogilvy's testing-driven craft discipline is the working corrective: every claim should be testable, every headline should have been beaten by a better one, every piece of long copy earns its length sentence by sentence. The methodology's `launch-brief` skill treats copy as a craft discipline, not a generation task. When the marketing-expert reviewer flags AI-drafted copy as "technically correct, professionally mediocre," Ogilvy is the citation.

## Key concepts the role pulls from

- **Testing as the arbiter.** Ogilvy's core discipline: you don't know which headline works until you test it. He tested rigorously and kept records. AI-generated copy skips this; the methodology flags it as unacceptable for high-stakes surfaces. The `launch-brief`'s copy-validation requirement is Ogilvy's testing ethic translated into a procedural step.
- **The headline is 80% of the ad.** Ogilvy's famous ratio: most readers see only the headline. A mediocre body with a great headline outperforms a great body with a mediocre headline. This makes headline craft the highest-leverage copy activity — and the place where AI defaults most visibly to generic formulations.
- **Long copy works when it earns it.** Ogilvy's counter-intuitive finding: long copy outperforms short copy for products where the reader has a real decision to make. The discipline is not "write long" but "every sentence earns the next." The `launch-brief`'s anti-pattern of padding is directly the failure Ogilvy's long-copy discipline guards against.
- **Research before rhetoric.** Ogilvy's pre-writing discipline: know the product deeply, know the buyer deeply, know the competition. "Advertising people who ignore research are as dangerous as generals who ignore decodes of enemy signals." The marketing-expert reviewer flags launch copy that reads like it was generated without customer research — which is the same violation.
- **The consumer is not a moron; she is your wife.** Ogilvy's most-quoted heuristic: do not condescend, do not oversimplify, do not write to the lowest common denominator. AI-generated copy regularly condescends through over-explained simplification. The `launch-brief`'s tone review catches this.
- **Positioning happens before copy.** Ogilvy insisted the advertising decision is determined before the copy is written — you must know who you're for and what you're claiming before a word is set. This aligns directly with Dunford's positioning-first discipline and makes Ogilvy an unexpected ally of the canvas's upstream-positioning requirement.

## What to actually read (1-hour budget)

- *Ogilvy on Advertising* — Chapters 1, 3, 4, and 7: what advertising does, how to write great ads, how to write great headlines, and how Ogilvy ran his own shop. These four chapters carry the craft load.
- *Confessions of an Advertising Man* — Chapter 1 ("How to Build Great Campaigns") and Chapter 10 ("Should Advertising Be Abolished?") — if you have a second hour. The book is older but the craft principles are sharper and more opinionated than *Ogilvy on Advertising*.

## What to skip

- *Ogilvy on Advertising*'s media-planning chapters are completely dated — the media landscape he describes no longer exists. Skip them.
- The "how to run an ad agency" chapters in *Confessions* are period documents and agency-management content; not load-bearing for the marketing-expert role's skills.

## Cross-role connection

Ogilvy's testing discipline pairs with the engineer's TDD discipline in an unexpected way: both insist on a falsifiable criterion before the artifact is shipped. The engineer writes a failing test before the function; Ogilvy runs an A/B before committing to the headline. The methodology's cross-role critique surfaces this alignment when the engineering-reviewer asks "what's the test?" and the marketing-expert reviewer asks "what's the headline variant?" The instinct is the same; the domain differs. Pair this note with `research/software-engineer/beck-tdd.md` when the team discusses launch copy testing protocols.
