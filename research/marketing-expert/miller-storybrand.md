# Donald Miller — distilled

> *Building a StoryBrand: Clarify Your Message So Customers Will Listen* (HarperCollins, 2017). An accessible narrative framework for brand messaging — especially strong for small teams without specialist marketing staff.

## Why it matters for AI-coding

AI-generated brand messaging is bloated. Asked to write a homepage, an AI produces seven paragraphs where two would do; asked for an about page, it produces a history of the company's founding where the customer's problem would be more relevant. Miller's SB7 framework forces concision through structure: seven required elements, each with a specific job, none of which is "describe our company." The discipline is not aesthetic — it's strategic: every word on the page should serve the customer's story, not the company's self-description. The `strategic-narrative` skill cites Miller for the customer-as-hero principle; the `launch-brief` uses SB7 for website and brand-messaging surfaces.

## Key concepts the role pulls from

- **Customer-as-hero.** Miller's foundational move, adapted from Joseph Campbell's hero's journey: the customer is the protagonist; the brand is the guide. Most brand messaging inverts this (the company is the hero; the customer is the beneficiary) and fails because readers don't care about the company's story — they care about their own. The `strategic-narrative` skill cites Miller directly for this principle; every narrative check includes a customer-as-hero test.
- **The SB7 framework.** Seven elements in order: (1) a character (the hero/customer) (2) has a problem (external, internal, philosophical) (3) meets a guide (the brand) (4) who gives them a plan (5) and calls them to action (6) that ends in success (7) or failure. The framework is a compression of the hero's journey into a brand-messaging structure. Powers the `strategic-narrative` skill's customer-as-hero checks and the `launch-brief`'s brand-messaging review.
- **Three levels of problem.** Miller's most useful elaboration: every customer problem exists at three levels — external (the practical problem), internal (how it makes them feel), and philosophical (why it's wrong that this problem exists). Great brand messaging addresses all three, not just the external. AI-generated copy addresses only the external. The `launch-brief`'s messaging-depth check is grounded here.
- **The guide's two qualities.** Miller's discipline for the brand-as-guide: the guide has (a) empathy and (b) authority. Empathy alone reads as soft; authority alone reads as arrogant. Both are required. The `launch-brief`'s tone review uses this frame to evaluate brand voice across customer-facing surfaces.
- **The plan removes paralysis.** Miller's insight on calls to action: customers often don't act not because they don't want the outcome, but because they don't know the steps. The plan (3-step or 4-step simple process) removes the fear of getting it wrong. AI-generated landing pages frequently omit this; the `launch-brief` mandates a plan section for any page with a conversion objective.

## What to actually read (1-hour budget)

- *Building a StoryBrand* — full read, ~200 pages, ~3-4 hours unhurried, ~1.5 hours focused. Miller writes accessibly with high repetition; you can skim the case-study chapters and get 90% of the framework from the framework chapters (1-9).
- The StoryBrand one-liner exercise — free at mystorybrand.com. 15 minutes of structured application that makes the framework concrete faster than re-reading the book.

## What to skip

- The workbook chapters at the end of the book — they duplicate the framework in exercise form. Useful for team workshops; redundant for individual study.
- The later StoryBrand/Business Made Simple series (Miller has extended the brand into business operations, email, and sales content) — tangential to the brand-messaging scope the Hornero marketing-expert role focuses on.

## Cross-role connection

Miller's narrative framework connects directly to the designer role's content-design concerns: both are concerned with the structure and sequence of information on a page, and both resist the company's instinct to lead with its own story. Where the designer asks "what does the user need to understand in what order?" Miller asks "what is the customer's story and how does the brand enter it?" The answers converge on customer-first, job-first information architecture rather than company-first. When the designer-reviewer and the marketing-expert-reviewer both flag a homepage as "all about the company," Miller and the designer role's content-design principles are the joint corrective. Pair this note with `research/designer/` content-design entries when working on brand-messaging surfaces.
