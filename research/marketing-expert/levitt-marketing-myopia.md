# Theodore Levitt — distilled

> "Marketing Myopia" (HBR, 1960; reprinted 2004) and *The Marketing Imagination* (1983). The foundational warning that companies define themselves by what they make rather than what their customers buy.

## Why it matters for AI-coding

AI-powered marketing tools default to the company's feature set as the unit of analysis — they summarise what the product does, not what the customer is trying to accomplish. This is marketing myopia in capital letters: the same failure Levitt diagnosed in the railroad and oil industries sixty years ago. An AI that generates copy from a product brief will reliably produce product-centred messaging unless the methodology forces a customer-need frame upstream. Levitt is the corrective: before any marketing artifact is generated, the methodology demands the team answer "what business are we actually in?"

## Key concepts the role pulls from

- **The customer-need frame.** "People don't buy drills, they buy holes." The unit of strategy is the customer's job, not the company's product. Every downstream marketing artifact — positioning canvas, narrative, launch brief — starts with the job before the product. Powers the `positioning-canvas` skill's competitive-alternatives component (what would the customer do if you didn't exist, i.e., what job would they hire instead?).
- **Industry myopia.** A company that defines its industry by its current product will be blindsided by substitutes that satisfy the same customer job via different means. Railroads defined themselves as railroad companies, not transportation companies; they lost to trucking and aviation. The marketing-expert reviewer flags this when a launch brief's category definition is too narrow.
- **Self-deceiving cycle.** Levitt's mechanism: success at selling a product leads to product-focus, which leads to ignoring shifts in what the customer actually needs, which leads to disruption. The mechanism is self-reinforcing and invisible from inside. The `pre-launch-marketing-audit` flags early-stage myopia before the cycle locks in.
- **Growth through customer imagination.** The corrective is not market research alone but imagination: asking "what would a customer hire *instead* of our product to get the same job done?" This is the question the positioning-canvas forces via competitive-alternatives.

## What to actually read (1-hour budget)

- "Marketing Myopia" — the 1960 HBR article is ~25 pages; the 2004 reprint includes a short retrospective by Levitt that is worth reading. The article itself is fast; the railroad and Hollywood examples are the load-bearing illustrations.
- *The Marketing Imagination*, Chapter 1 — "Marketing and Its Discontents" — is the most direct translation of the article's ideas into a practitioner frame. Skip the rest of the book unless you want the full Levitt voice.

## What to skip

- The 1960 article's petroleum-industry section is dated; the mechanism it illustrates is valid but the examples are no longer fresh. Abstract the principle; don't cite the specific industry analysis.
- Most of *The Marketing Imagination* beyond Chapter 1 — it covers sales management, globalization, and innovation topics that are tangential to the role's skills. Time is better spent on Dunford and Ritson for contemporary depth.

## Cross-role connection

Levitt is JTBD's grandparent — Clayton Christensen cites "Marketing Myopia" directly as the ancestor of jobs-to-be-done theory. The product-manager role's `jobs-to-be-done` skill and the marketing-expert role's `positioning-canvas` share this lineage. When the PM and marketing-expert reviewers both flag a feature-first brief, Levitt and Christensen are the joint ancestors of that critique. The framing is the same from different angles: Christensen asks "what job is the customer hiring this for?" and Levitt asks "what business are we actually in?" Both questions force the team away from the product and toward the customer's situation. Pair this note with `research/product-manager/christensen-jtbd.md` when the team is working across both roles on a positioning or launch decision.
