# Daniel Kahneman — distilled

> *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011). *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* (with Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein, 2021). The foundational vocabulary for cognitive bias and decision noise, from the Nobel laureate who spent fifty years mapping the systematic ways human judgment departs from rationality.

## Why it matters for AI-coding

AI tools translate human biases into automated decisions at scale. The bias is not in the AI — it is in the training data, the labelling decisions, the metric choices, the framing of the analytical question, and the interpretation of the results. All of those human decisions are subject to exactly the biases Kahneman catalogued: anchoring (the first number seen shapes all subsequent estimates), availability (what comes to mind easily is treated as representative), confirmation (evidence consistent with the prior hypothesis is weighted more heavily), and the planning fallacy (projects take longer and cost more than estimated because the distribution of outcomes is not consulted). Kahneman gives the data scientist the vocabulary to name these biases in their own decisions — not in the model, not in the user, but in the analyst's own interpretation of what the data means.

*Noise* extends the argument: variance in human judgment (different reviewers giving different scores to the same case) is as large a problem as bias (all reviewers systematically wrong in the same direction), but it is almost never measured. AI systems trained on noisy labels learn the noise as signal. Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein give the vocabulary to design noise-reduction interventions before training begins, not after the model is deployed.

## Key concepts the role pulls from

- **System 1 and System 2.** Fast, automatic, pattern-matching (System 1) vs. slow, effortful, deliberate reasoning (System 2). Data scientists use System 2 to build models and System 1 to interpret outputs — and System 1 is wrong in predictable ways. The tension between the two systems is the practitioner's bias-awareness problem: the model gave a number; does the number feel right? "Feeling right" is System 1, and System 1 is what produces the biases. Powers the role's "does this result pass the smell test?" critique: smell tests are System 1 checks, and Kahneman is the reason to distrust them without a System 2 audit.
- **Anchoring.** The first estimate or reference point in an analysis contaminates all subsequent judgments. When an AI produces a preliminary result and then the analyst refines it, the refinement is anchored to the AI's output. Powers the `experiment-design` pre-registration requirement: the analysis plan must be written before the data is seen, or anchoring has already corrupted the question.
- **Availability heuristic.** Frequency is estimated by ease of recall. A model evaluation that focuses on memorable failure cases is not a representative sample of failure. A dashboard that highlights recent anomalies creates availability bias in the team's sense of model reliability. Powers the `pre-launch-measurement-audit` representative-sampling requirement: the evaluation set must be constructed to be representative, not memorable.
- **Regression to the mean.** Extreme observations are followed by less extreme ones — not because of any intervention, but because of sampling variance. Kahneman's example: flight instructors who praised good landings saw worse performance next time; those who criticised bad landings saw better — not because praise is bad but because extreme performance regresses. Product teams intervene on outliers and credit the intervention. Powers the `causal-dag` confounding critique: did the intervention cause the improvement, or did the extreme case regress regardless?
- **Planning fallacy.** Estimates of project duration and cost are biased toward best-case scenarios because planners focus on their own plan rather than the distribution of outcomes for similar projects (the "inside view"). The reference-class forecast (the "outside view") is more accurate. Powers the data-scientist's evaluation timeline and data-collection estimates: always consult reference-class distributions, not just the current project's apparent trajectory.
- **Noise (variability in judgment).** Two reviewers labelling the same item give different labels not because one is biased but because the task itself is underspecified and judgment is variable. Noise audit: measure inter-rater reliability before training on human labels. If noise is high, the labels are not trustworthy as training signal. Powers the `pre-launch-measurement-audit`'s label-quality check for supervised models.

## What to actually read (1-hour budget)

- ***Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Part II (Heuristics and Biases), Chapters 11-18.** The core bias catalogue: anchoring, availability, representativeness, overconfidence, and the planning fallacy. These seven chapters are the primary vocabulary reference for the role.
- ***Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Chapter 33** (reversals) and **Chapter 34** (frames and reality) — the two chapters on how framing effects contaminate judgment. Critical for understanding why the same statistical result reported differently produces different decisions.
- ***Noise*, Chapter 2** (measuring noise in human judgment) and **Chapter 17** (decision hygiene) — the noise diagnosis and the remediation protocol. The rest of *Noise* extends the argument; these two chapters are the load-bearing content.

## What to skip

- *Thinking, Fast and Slow* Part V (Two Selves) — philosophically interesting, not load-bearing for the data-scientist role's practical bias-awareness work.
- *Noise*'s extended case studies in medicine and law — illustrative but slow-paced; the core insight (noise is measured differently from bias and requires different interventions) is in Chapter 2.

## How this anchors role skills

- [`pre-launch-measurement-audit`](../../roles/data-scientist/skills/pre-launch-measurement-audit/SKILL.md) — the bias-awareness and label-quality checks draw from Kahneman's vocabulary. The audit asks: were labelling instructions specific enough to reduce noise? were evaluation sets constructed to avoid availability bias? were project timelines estimated with reference-class forecasts?
- [`metric-definition`](../../roles/data-scientist/skills/metric-definition/SKILL.md) — the anchoring and framing effects are load-bearing in the gaming-surface analysis: a metric presented in a specific frame (e.g., "retention is 70%" vs. "churn is 30%") produces different decisions because of framing effects, not because the underlying reality differs.
- [`experiment-design`](../../roles/data-scientist/skills/experiment-design/SKILL.md) — planning fallacy and anchoring both argue for pre-registration: the analysis plan must be written before the data is seen, and the MDE must be set before the effect size is observed.

## Cross-role connection

Kahneman's bias vocabulary has the widest cross-role reach of any anchor in the data-scientist bibliography. The marketing expert's behavioural-economics framing (Sutherland, Cialdini, Ariely) draws from the same bias catalogue — availability, anchoring, social proof, and loss aversion all appear in both. When the methodology's `multi-role-review` pairs a data scientist and a marketing expert, Kahneman is the shared theoretical substrate. The PM decision-making under uncertainty (Torres, Cagan) is also Kahneman-adjacent: the planning fallacy is the canonical failure mode of product roadmaps. And the security expert's threat-modelling discipline — adversaries exploit cognitive biases, not just technical vulnerabilities — is richer with Kahneman's vocabulary for which biases get exploited first (availability: we defend against memorable attacks; anchoring: initial threat assessments shape all subsequent ones).
