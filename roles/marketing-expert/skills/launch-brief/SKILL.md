# Launch brief

> v0 draft. Grounded in Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) on penetration vs. loyalty and mental/physical availability, April Dunford's *Sales Pitch* (2023) on cascading positioning across surfaces, Mark Ritson's strategic-then-tactical sequencing, Bob Hoffman on evidence-based channel choice, and the Google SRE release-engineering tradition for the audit-and-readiness discipline.

## Purpose

Produce a launch brief that:

1. **Anchors to positioning and narrative** — the brief is a downstream artifact, never a substitute for those.
2. **Names the audience precisely** — including the buyer, the user, the champion, and the economic decision-maker if they differ.
3. **Sequences channels** — what fires when, against what audience, with what asset, to what end.
4. **States KPIs honestly** — with attribution methodology, range estimates, and the awareness-vs-activation distinction made explicit.
5. **Defines out-of-scope** — what the launch is not trying to accomplish; pre-empts scope creep.
6. **Plans the post-launch readout** — what we'll learn and how we'll know.

The brief is the artifact that prevents launches from collapsing into "let's run an ad, post on LinkedIn, and email the list." It forces strategic intent into operational form.

A launch brief without measurement design is wishful thinking. A launch brief without attribution honesty is performance theatre. A launch brief without "what this is NOT" is scope creep waiting to happen. The skill exists to resist all three.

## Key Concepts

### Sharp's distinction: penetration vs. loyalty; mental vs. physical availability

Byron Sharp (*How Brands Grow*, Ehrenberg-Bass Institute): brand growth comes mostly from **penetration** (reaching more buyers in the category) rather than **loyalty** (extracting more from existing buyers). Two distinct disciplines support penetration:

- **Mental availability.** The brand is thought of in buying situations. Built by reach, distinctive assets, and consistent exposure over time.
- **Physical availability.** The brand is easy to find and buy when the buying situation arises. Built by distribution, channel presence, and friction reduction.

The launch brief sequences activities against both. Awareness work builds mental availability (slow, compounding); activation work converts in-market buyers (fast, measurable). Confusing these is the dominant marketing-measurement failure mode.

### Awareness vs. activation distinction

- **Awareness work** builds mental availability over time. Hard to attribute directly; KPI is reach, frequency, and recall of distinctive assets. Sharp's research: measurable returns appear over quarters, not weeks.
- **Activation work** converts in-market buyers now. Easier to attribute; KPI is lead generation, signup, demo, pipeline. Returns appear in weeks.

A launch brief that uses awareness-shape spend (broad-reach, brand-building) and measures with activation KPIs (last-click conversion) will report failure regardless of whether the work succeeded. The brief states which mode each activity is in.

### Ritson's strategic-then-tactical sequence

Mark Ritson's diagnostic discipline: marketing decisions cascade from diagnosis → strategy → tactics. Most launches skip diagnosis and start at tactics ("let's run an ad campaign"). The brief enforces order:

- **Diagnosis** — who's buying, why, what they're comparing to, what triggers buying. (Largely settled by the positioning canvas; the brief inherits.)
- **Strategy** — where to play (segments / channels) and how to win (the wedge into those segments).
- **Tactics** — specific channels, assets, sequences, dates, owners.

The brief contains all three; if any is missing, the brief is premature.

### Hoffman on evidence-based channel choice

Bob Hoffman ("The Ad Contrarian"): channel performance is heavily context-dependent. The channel mix that worked at a previous company will not necessarily work here. Industry fashion ("everyone's doing X") is the worst input. The brief states the *evidence* behind each channel choice:

- Existing analytics from prior campaigns / similar segments.
- Public benchmarks for the channel in the audience's category.
- Test learnings from pre-launch experiments.
- Customer-acquisition pattern from peer companies (where reachable through advisors / case studies).

Channels chosen without evidence get marked as exploratory and treated with smaller budgets and clearer learning goals.

### Attribution honesty

Marketing attribution data is noisier and more biased than dashboards admit:

- **Last-click** under-attributes awareness work.
- **First-click** under-attributes activation work.
- **Multi-touch** is configurable enough that the "right" answer is essentially the modeler's choice.
- **MMM (Marketing Mix Modeling)** improves directionally but has lag and variance.
- **Self-report** (e.g., "How did you hear about us?") captures top-of-mind, not full path.

The brief states which method is used, which biases apply, and reports KPIs as **ranges with assumptions**, not point estimates. "Launch generated 800-1,200 pipeline opportunities (CRM-attributed, last-click, with a ±25% confidence interval reflecting cross-channel attribution noise)" is honest; "Launch generated 1,047 pipeline opportunities" is not.

### Distinctive assets compound; novelty doesn't

Sharp on distinctive assets: brand logos, colors, sounds, taglines, characters that get used consistently over time build memory structures buyers recognise. Every campaign that reinvents the visual language wastes the prior campaign's accumulation. The launch brief states which distinctive assets are deployed and confirms consistency with prior work.

### Out-of-scope is load-bearing

Most launches over-scope. The brief names:

- Segments the launch is *not* targeting (even though they have the problem).
- Channels the launch is *not* using (even though they're available).
- Geographies the launch is *not* covering (even though there's appetite).
- Outcomes the launch is *not* trying to deliver (e.g., "this launch is for mental availability; pipeline is a downstream effect we'll measure at +90 days, not at +14 days").

Pre-empting scope creep is the difference between launches that ship on time and launches that ship "next quarter, just one more channel."

## Application

### Step 1 — Confirm positioning + narrative are approved

The launch brief is third in the cascade: positioning ([`positioning-canvas`](../positioning-canvas/SKILL.md)) → narrative ([`strategic-narrative`](../strategic-narrative/SKILL.md)) → brief. If either upstream artifact is missing or stale, pause the brief and finish upstream.

### Step 2 — Decompose the audience

Even when positioning's "who-cares-most" is clear, the launch may need to address multiple roles:

- **Buyer.** The person who signs.
- **User.** The person who uses daily.
- **Champion.** The internal advocate who triggers the buying process.
- **Economic decision-maker.** The budget holder.
- **Influencers.** Advisors, analysts, peers, communities that shape the buyer's perception.

For each role, name how the launch reaches them. The same narrative; different surfaces, different messaging variants.

### Step 3 — Set the strategic shape

State explicitly:

- **Primary mode** — is this launch primarily *awareness* (mental availability) or *activation* (pipeline / signup)?
- **Time horizon** — when do we expect the primary KPIs to land? (Awareness work: quarters. Activation work: weeks.)
- **Distinctive assets in play** — which existing assets get used, which new ones get introduced.
- **The wedge** — what specific segment or use-case is the launch trying to land first? (Mirrors who-cares-most from positioning.)

### Step 4 — Choose channels with evidence

For each candidate channel, state:

- **Why this channel** — audience-fit evidence; not industry fashion.
- **What asset** — the format and the message variant.
- **What KPI** — awareness-shape (reach / recall / share-of-voice) or activation-shape (clicks / signups / pipeline).
- **What attribution** — how we'll know it worked (or didn't).
- **What budget** — and what we'd cut if budget shrunk.

Channels without evidence become small exploratory bets with clear learning goals — not pulled out, but right-sized.

### Step 5 — Sequence the launch

Most launches are not a single moment; they are 2-8 weeks of phased work. State the sequence:

- **Pre-launch phase** — embargoed press, analyst briefings, sales-team enablement, customer-story preparation.
- **Launch moment** — the single public moment (announcement, demo day, conference talk). What fires?
- **Sustain phase** — what runs in the weeks after the launch moment to compound mental and physical availability.
- **Iteration window** — when we'll re-evaluate and adjust based on early signal.

### Step 6 — Plan measurement honestly

For each KPI, state:

- The metric.
- The baseline (what the number is now, before the launch).
- The target (where we expect it to land, as a range).
- The horizon (when we expect to read it).
- The attribution methodology and its biases.
- The confidence we have in the measurement.

The measurement design is reviewed by data-scientist before launch — the marketing-expert proposes; data-scientist confirms validity (or surfaces issues).

### Step 8 — Validate against eight checks

1. **Positioning + narrative anchor check.** The brief references both upstream artifacts and inherits from them; doesn't restate or contradict.
2. **Primary-mode declared check.** Awareness vs. activation is stated; KPIs match.
3. **Audience decomposition check.** Buyer, user, champion, economic decision-maker, influencers are distinguished where they differ.
4. **Channel-evidence check.** Each channel has stated evidence; channels without evidence are marked exploratory with small budgets and learning goals.
5. **Attribution-honesty check.** KPIs are ranges with stated methods and biases, not point estimates.
6. **Distinctive-asset continuity check.** The launch reuses existing distinctive assets; new ones are deliberate decisions, not accident.
7. **Out-of-scope check.** At least one audience, one channel, and one outcome are explicitly out.
8. **Measurement-design check.** Data-scientist has reviewed the measurement plan and confirmed it's valid (or named issues).

### Step 9 — Walk with founders / CRO / CEO / data-scientist

The launch brief is reviewed with the people whose work it sets in motion:

- **Founders / CEO.** Strategic shape, out-of-scope decisions.
- **CRO / Head of Sales.** Sales enablement, activation expectations, lead-quality assumptions.
- **Data-scientist.** Measurement design validity.
- **Product-manager.** Launch-date dependencies on product-readiness.
- **Designer.** Asset production schedule and distinctive-asset continuity.

Objections logged; revisions tracked.

### Step 10 — Post-launch readout

The brief plans the readout artifact in advance:

- **T+30 readout.** Early signal; activation KPIs starting to read; awareness KPIs not yet meaningful.
- **T+90 readout.** Awareness KPIs becoming readable; activation pipeline mature enough to assess quality.
- **T+180 readout.** Compounding effects (mental availability, pipeline lifecycle) measurable; lessons feed the next launch's positioning refresh.

Each readout updates the brief's KPI table with actuals; lessons feed positioning, narrative, and next-launch brief.

## Examples

### Example 1 — A disciplined launch brief (good, abridged)

```markdown
# Launch brief — LedgerStack v2 GA launch

Status: Approved. Launch: 2026-02-04.
Primary mode: Mixed — 60% awareness (building category mental
```

A reviewer can argue with budget percentages, KPI ranges, channel choices — they can verify the evidence basis for each. The brief is falsifiable, which is the point.

### Example 2 — Failure mode (bad)

```markdown
# Launch brief — v2
- Run a campaign on LinkedIn + email blast + Twitter / X.
- Get the team excited.
- Goal: more leads.
- Budget: $X.
```

Failures: no audience decomposition; no positioning/narrative linkage; no mode declaration; no attribution; no out-of-scope; "more leads" is not a KPI; no sequence; no readout plan. The launch will run; the team will not learn from it.

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1 — Tactics-first launch

**Symptom:** Brief opens with channel choices and asset list; positioning and audience are missing or hand-waved.

**Consequence:** Ritson's diagnosis: tactics chosen without diagnosis and strategy. The launch goes through motion without coherent intent.

**Fix:** Brief inherits from positioning + narrative; channel choices justified per Hoffman's evidence-based discipline.

### Pitfall 2 — Awareness work measured by activation KPIs

**Symptom:** Brief spends 60% on broad-reach awareness; KPIs are last-click signup attribution.

**Consequence:** Awareness work reports as "failure" regardless of whether it built mental availability. Future awareness budget gets cut on the strength of mis-measured numbers.

**Fix:** State primary mode explicitly. Awareness work measured by awareness KPIs (recall, share-of-voice, brand-search lift); activation work by activation KPIs (pipeline, signup, conversion).

### Pitfall 3 — Point-estimate KPIs

**Symptom:** "Launch will generate 1,200 leads."

**Consequence:** Forecasts present as more certain than they are. Misses surface as broken trust rather than as predicted variance.

**Fix:** Ranges with stated assumptions. "800-1,400 leads (CRM last-click, ±25% confidence)" preserves credibility on both sides.

### Pitfall 4 — Trend-chasing channels

**Symptom:** Brief allocates 30% to a fashionable channel ("TikTok") for an audience the channel doesn't reach.

**Consequence:** Hoffman's diagnosis: industry fashion produces channel choice; results disappoint; nobody learns because the next fashion arrives.

**Fix:** Each channel has stated evidence basis. Trend-flavoured channels get small exploratory budgets with learning goals, not large bets.

### Pitfall 5 — Distinctive-asset reinvention

**Symptom:** Launch introduces new visual language unconnected to prior brand work.

**Consequence:** Sharp's diagnosis: distinctive assets that compound get wasted; mental availability doesn't accumulate; every campaign feels different.

**Fix:** Audit distinctive assets in the brief. Existing assets reused; new assets justified by deliberate decision, not by creative team's preference for novelty.

### Pitfall 6 — No out-of-scope

**Symptom:** Every segment, every channel, every outcome is "in scope" or unspecified.

**Consequence:** Launch sprawls. Sales gets confused signal about lead quality. Measurement gets diluted. Ship date slips because "just one more channel" keeps being added.

**Fix:** Explicit out-of-scope section. Some segments / channels / outcomes are deliberately excluded; reviewers can argue but cannot ignore.

### Pitfall 7 — Measurement design as afterthought

**Symptom:** KPIs are written by marketing without data-scientist review; instrumentation is tested at T-7 and breaks.

**Consequence:** Launch goes live; data is unreadable; the readout is "we think it worked but the dashboard's broken." Costly across multiple dimensions.

**Fix:** Data-scientist review at T-21 minimum; instrumentation tested at T-14; backup-attribution plan if primary fails.

### Pitfall 8 — No post-launch readout planned

**Symptom:** Brief ends at the launch moment; no T+30 / T+90 / T+180 readout artifacts planned.

**Consequence:** No one writes the readout. Lessons never compound into the next launch. The next launch repeats the same failure modes.

**Fix:** Readout schedule and ownership stated in the brief itself, with named decision triggers (e.g., "if pipeline target missed by >30% at T+45, reallocate from awareness to activation").

### Pitfall 9 — Single-source brief

**Symptom:** CMO authors the brief; CRO, CEO, data-scientist haven't reviewed.

**Consequence:** Launch is misaligned with sales capacity, board expectations, or measurement reality. Mid-launch reset becomes necessary.

**Fix:** Stakeholder review explicit. Each named reviewer must sign off (or formally object) before brief is "Approved."
