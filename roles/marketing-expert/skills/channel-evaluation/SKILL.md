# Channel evaluation

> v0 draft. Grounded in Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) on penetration, mental availability, and reach, Mark Ritson's strategic-then-tactical sequencing discipline (Marketing Week), Bob Hoffman's *The Ad Contrarian* essays on evidence-based channel choice and attribution honesty, Rory Sutherland's *Alchemy* (2019) on channel-as-framing-context, and David Ogilvy's *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983) on craft-must-fit-medium.

## Purpose

Produce a channel readiness scorecard that:

1. **Evaluates audience fit** — is the target buyer actually reachable on this channel, and do they use it in a buying-receptive state?
2. **Evaluates team capacity** — can the team produce content at the channel's cadence, at the channel's quality bar, sustainably?
3. **Evaluates measurement honesty** — can the channel's contribution be stated without false confidence? If not, what can be said?
4. **Evaluates opportunity cost** — what is foregone by choosing this channel over its best alternative use of the same budget and effort?

Channel decisions that skip this evaluation tend to be driven by three forces, all of them unreliable: **industry fashion** (everyone's doing it), **founder intuition** (I personally use this channel), and **vendor salesmanship** (the channel platform's own case studies). The scorecard makes these forces visible so the team can weigh them deliberately.

The AI-era addition: AI marketing tools recommend channels based on attribution data from their training sets. That data systematically over-attributes to measurable, last-click channels (paid search, email) and under-attributes to channels with diffuse effect (community, podcast, event, PR). Hoffman's framing is the essential corrective: attribution data tells you what you can measure, not what is working.

## Key Concepts

### Sharp: reach follows the buyer, not the brand

Byron Sharp (*How Brands Grow*): brands grow primarily through **penetration** — reaching more buyers in the category — not through deeper engagement with existing buyers. Channel decisions should be evaluated against one primary question: **does this channel reach the buyers I haven't reached yet?**

Two availability types the channel must serve:

- **Mental availability.** The brand is thought of when a buying situation arises. Built by reaching buyers repeatedly with consistent, memorable signals before they are in the market. Most channels contribute to this; slow to measure; essential to brand growth.
- **Physical availability.** The brand is easy to find and buy when the buying moment arrives. Built by distribution, channel presence, ease of trial. Often confused with conversion-rate optimization; actually about removing friction from the buying path.

A channel that reaches only existing customers does not build penetration. A channel that reaches the right new buyers even if it can't be attributed directly may still be the highest-leverage investment.

### Ritson: channel is execution, not strategy

Mark Ritson's strategic sequencing: diagnosis → strategy → tactics. A channel is a tactic. The sequence means: **the channel decision cannot be made before the strategic question is answered.**

Before evaluating a channel, confirm:

- **What is the strategic objective?** Build brand awareness in a new segment? Convert in-market buyers? Retain and expand existing customers? Each objective implies a different channel profile.
- **Who is the target segment?** Channel fit is segment-specific, not universal. LinkedIn may be right for CFO-targeting and wrong for SMB sole traders buying the same product.
- **What is the posture — push or pull?** Push channels (paid social, outbound email, display) interrupt. Pull channels (search, community, referral) attract. Strategy determines which posture the moment calls for.

A team that picks a channel before answering these questions has skipped strategy. The channel evaluation begins by surfacing whether strategy is settled.

### Hoffman: attribution is a claim, not a fact

Bob Hoffman's central discipline: the channel that gets credited in your dashboard is not necessarily the channel that drove the decision. Attribution models are tools for making an argument, not for measuring reality.

Common attribution errors:

- **Last-click overcount.** The final touch before conversion gets all the credit; the five earlier touches that built conviction are invisible. Paid search benefits enormously from this.
- **Correlated vs. causal.** Email open-rate and purchase intent may be correlated because the same engaged segment does both; email didn't cause the purchase intent.
- **Brand recall invisibility.** Community presence, podcast appearances, PR coverage, and event sponsorships build recall that precedes and influences search and direct visits — but because they don't generate trackable clicks, they don't appear in the attribution model.
- **AI-attribution pitfall.** AI-powered channel-optimization tools train on historical attribution data and thus inherit its biases. A tool that recommends scaling paid search over podcast sponsorship may be recommending what's measurable, not what's working.

The scorecard names what attribution *can* honestly claim and what it cannot. A channel's inability to be attributed directly is not evidence against it; it is a measurement design problem to be solved separately.

### Sutherland: channel as framing context

Rory Sutherland (*Alchemy*): the context in which a message is received changes how it is interpreted, independent of the message's content. A brand appearing in a respected industry newsletter carries implicit endorsement from the publication's authority. The same brand running a programmatic display ad carries no such endorsement — the channel's context actively signals "we paid to be here." Channel choice is framing choice.

Sutherland's implication for channel evaluation: the *where* of a message is part of the message. An enterprise security product in a pop-up display ad sends a different signal than the same product in a CSO community roundtable. Evaluate not just "does the audience see this channel" but "what does appearing in this channel signal about the brand?"

### Ogilvy: craft must fit the medium

David Ogilvy's practitioner discipline: each channel has its own conventions, its own cadence, its own craft standards. A billboard is a headline and an image; it cannot carry long copy. A magazine ad can carry long copy; tested against direct mail, longer copy outperforms shorter in print. A 30-second TV spot is character and emotion; a search ad is answer and intent.

The evaluation asks: does the team have the craft capacity for this channel's conventions? A team that produces strong long-form written content may not have the visual-editing craft for organic social. A team that's strong on written thought leadership may produce weak podcast episodes. Craft mismatch produces weak creative that wastes channel reach.

## Application

### Step 1 — Confirm the strategic frame

Before evaluating any channel, state:

- **Strategic objective** (awareness / consideration / conversion / retention — named specifically).
- **Target segment** (ICP from the positioning canvas, or your best inference).
- **Stage** (pre-launch, launch, sustain, expansion — the channel profile that's appropriate changes with stage).
- **Posture** (push or pull, or a stated mix).

If the strategic frame is unclear, the first scorecard output is: "Strategic objective unstated — channel cannot be evaluated for fit until it is."

### Step 2 — Score on four dimensions

For each candidate channel, produce scores (1 = weak, 3 = moderate, 5 = strong) with a rationale sentence per dimension.

**Dimension 1 — Audience fit** (Sharp)

Questions to answer:
- Is the target segment present on this channel at material scale?
- Are they in a *buying-receptive* state when using it? (A CFO doom-scrolling LinkedIn at 6 AM is in a different state than the same CFO searching "multi-entity accounting software comparison" on Google.)
- Does the channel's demographic and behavioral profile match the segment, or is it adjacent?
- Is there evidence of audience presence (platform demographics, peer-company case studies, anecdotal reports from sales) or only an assumption?

**Dimension 2 — Team capacity** (Ogilvy craft; operational reality)

Questions to answer:
- What is this channel's minimum-viable cadence? (SEO needs months of consistent output; paid search can go live this week; podcast is fortnightly minimum; LinkedIn organic is 3-5x/week for meaningful reach.)
- Does the team have the craft skills this channel's conventions require? (Writing? Video editing? Audio production? Graphic design?)
- Who specifically would own it? Is that person's capacity actually available?
- What would be deprioritized to create this capacity?

Score honestly. A channel that's a good fit for the audience but would require a new hire to execute is a different bet than a channel the existing team can operate well.

**Dimension 3 — Measurement honesty** (Hoffman)

Questions to answer:
- What *can* honestly be attributed to this channel? (Click-throughs and conversion from UTM-tagged links? Brand recall from survey? Pipeline from CRM cohort analysis? None of the above?)
- What *cannot* be attributed? (Awareness contribution? Long-latency brand influence?)
- What is the minimum evidence that would tell us whether this channel is working?
- Is there a risk of false confidence — reporting this channel as "driving X" when it's correlated with, not causing, X?

A channel that cannot be attributed at all is not automatically wrong (Sharp's mental-availability channels often can't be attributed directly). But the team must be clear about what evidence they are collecting and what conclusions it supports.

**Dimension 4 — Opportunity cost** (Ritson; budget discipline)

Questions to answer:
- What is the best alternative use of the same budget and team hours?
- What would be cut or slowed if this channel were added?
- Is the channel a bet on reach (new audience) or a bet on conversion (existing audience)? Does that match the stage?
- If this channel underperforms, what is the cost to exit? (Paid channels: close the tap. SEO: sunk cost in months of content. Community: reputational cost of going dark.)

### Step 4 — Channel-mix view (optional)

If evaluating multiple channels simultaneously (channel audit), produce a comparison table before the individual scorecards:

```markdown
| Channel | Audience fit | Capacity | Measurement | Opportunity cost | Total | Rec |
|---------|-------------|----------|-------------|-----------------|-------|-----|
| <ch1> | | | | | /20 | |
| <ch2> | | | | | /20 | |
```

The mix view surfaces prioritization: which channel to enter first, which to scale, which to cut.

### Step 5 — Check for the AI-attribution pitfall

Before finalizing the recommendation, ask: would an AI marketing tool recommend the opposite of this scorecard? If it would (because the recommended channel has weak last-click attribution), examine the recommendation carefully. The AI tool's recommendation reflects what's measurable in its training data. The scorecard's recommendation reflects strategic fit. These can conflict. Name the conflict explicitly if it exists.

## Examples

### Example 1 — Channel scorecard: podcast sponsorship for a B2B scale-up CFO audience

**Context:** LedgerStack, multi-entity accounting platform, evaluating podcast sponsorship on two finance-focused podcasts (*The CFO Podcast*, *20 Growth Finance Edition*). Strategic objective: brand awareness among Series A-C CFOs not yet in-market.

```markdown
# Channel evaluation scorecard — Podcast sponsorship (CFO-audience podcasts)

Strategic objective: Mental availability — build brand awareness among Series A-C CFOs before they enter active buying mode.
Target segment: Head of Finance / CFO, 2-15 entity scale-up, Series A-C.
Stage: Sustain (brand building alongside an active launch).

## Scores

| Dimension | Score | Rationale |
|-----------|-------|-----------|
| Audience fit | 4 | CFO Podcast and 20 Growth Finance Edition both report 60-70% CFO/VP Finance audience at growth-stage companies. Segment is present. Listening is habitual (commute, gym) — not purchase-mode, but brand-receptive. Two sales-call mentions of "I heard about LedgerStack on a podcast" in last quarter (weak but directional). |
| Team capacity | 3 | Pre-recorded host-read ad; production overhead is minimal (copy, talking points). Creative review burden is low. Risk: one team member needed to manage sponsor relationship and provide talking points per episode. Half-FTE at full schedule. Manageable but must be explicit. |
| Measurement honesty | 2 | Attribution is weak by design. Host-read ads don't carry UTM links; branded search lift is the best proxy; self-report ("how did you hear about us") is imprecise. Cannot claim pipeline attribution with confidence. Can claim: branded search lift (before/after), brand recall in quarterly survey, and self-report rate. Cannot claim: "podcast drove $X pipeline." |
| Opportunity cost | 3 | Same $18k/quarter could fund 200 LinkedIn impressions at scale-up CFO targeting — measurable, attributable, but more intrusive. Alternative gives measurable activation; podcast gives unmeasurable awareness. Given the strategic objective is awareness, the podcast is the better fit for the objective — but the opportunity cost of measurability is real and must be accepted explicitly. |
| **Total** | **12/20** | |

## Recommendation: Conditional

**Go condition:** Confirm audience fit with a small 8-week test (2 episodes); measure branded search lift and self-report rate; if either moves >15%, expand.

**What must be accepted:** Measurement will not produce pipeline attribution. The CMO and CEO must agree that "awareness investment without direct pipeline attribution" is acceptable before committing. If they cannot, this channel is wrong not because it doesn't work but because the measurement culture won't accept what it produces.
```

### Example 2 — Channel-mix comparison (abridged)

| Channel | Audience fit | Capacity | Measurement | Opp. cost | Total | Rec |
|---------|-------------|----------|-------------|-----------|-------|-----|
| LinkedIn sponsored content (CFO filter) | 4 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 15/20 | Go |
| Podcast sponsorship (2 CFO pods) | 4 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 12/20 | Conditional |

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1 — Channel chosen before strategy

**Symptom:** The team evaluates "should we do TikTok?" without first stating what strategic objective the channel would serve.

**Consequence:** Ritson's diagnosis: tactics chosen without diagnosis and strategy. The channel will be evaluated on the wrong terms.

**Fix:** Step 1 is non-negotiable. Strategic objective, target segment, and stage must be stated before any dimension is scored.

### Pitfall 2 — Measuring the wrong thing as success

**Symptom:** A brand-awareness channel (podcast, community, PR) is measured by last-click conversions; it "fails" and gets cut.

**Consequence:** The channel's actual contribution to mental availability is invisible. The team cuts the channel that was building brand and scales the channel that was converting the brand's built-up demand.

**Fix:** Measurement honesty scores the channel's attributability honestly. Awareness channels are evaluated on awareness metrics; activation channels on conversion metrics. Mixing them produces the wrong conclusion.

### Pitfall 3 — Capacity scored optimistically

**Symptom:** Capacity is scored as "we could do this" without naming who specifically would own it and what would be deprioritized.

**Consequence:** The channel gets approved; execution is poor because no one actually had the capacity; the channel "doesn't work."

**Fix:** Name the owner, the hours required per week at minimum viable cadence, and what would be cut or slowed. If the answer is "we don't know," the capacity score is 2, not 4.

### Pitfall 4 — Ignoring exit cost

**Symptom:** Opportunity cost dimension only evaluates the budget; ignores exit cost when the channel underperforms.

**Consequence:** The team commits to a channel with a high exit cost (SEO content; community relationships; event presence) without accounting for the cost of exiting if it doesn't work.

**Fix:** Exit cost is an explicit question in the opportunity-cost dimension.

### Pitfall 5 — Following the competitor

**Symptom:** "Competitor X just launched a [channel] program — we should too."

**Consequence:** Competitor X may have a different audience, different team capacity, different stage, or different measurement standards. Their channel choice tells you almost nothing about whether it's right for you.

**Fix:** Apply the scorecard regardless of what competitors are doing. Competitor channel moves surface in the opportunity-cost dimension if they signal a genuine audience-fit shift — not as a reason to follow.

### Pitfall 6 — AI-tool recommendation accepted without scrutiny

**Symptom:** A marketing AI tool or attribution platform recommends scaling Channel A and cutting Channel B. The team accepts the recommendation without examining the attribution model.

**Consequence:** Hoffman's pitfall: the recommendation reflects what's measurable, not what's working. If Channel B is building brand awareness with weak attribution, cutting it based on an AI recommendation may be cutting what's actually driving Channel A's conversions.

**Fix:** Apply the measurement-honesty dimension explicitly to AI-generated recommendations. Ask: what attribution model is this recommendation based on? What channels are systematically under-attributed in that model?
