# Strategic narrative

> v0 draft. Grounded in Andy Raskin's strategic-narrative methodology (his published essays at andyraskin.com and conference talks), April Dunford's *Sales Pitch* (2023), Donald Miller's *Building a StoryBrand* (2017), and the classical story-structure tradition (Aristotle through Christopher Booker through Aaron Sorkin).

## Purpose

Author a strategic narrative — *one* 90-second story — that:

1. **Names a shift in the world** that has changed what's possible or what's required.
2. **Shows winners and losers** of that shift, framing high stakes around adapting vs. not.
3. **Teases the promised land** — the future state for those who adapt successfully.
4. **Introduces capabilities** — what's needed to reach the promised land. (This is where the product enters.)
5. **Presents evidence** — proof the capabilities work, ideally including named customers.

The narrative is *not* the elevator pitch, the value prop, the positioning, or the homepage copy. It is the *story* that those derive from. The same narrative powers the investor deck, the keynote, the sales pitch's opening, the careers-page mission paragraph, and the home page's hero section. When the narrative is sharp, everything downstream becomes easier to write.

When the narrative is missing or muddled, downstream artifacts fragment — every team produces its own opening, voice, framing — and the company sounds like five companies. Raskin's central observation: most companies don't have a strategic narrative; they have a *feature pitch dressed up*.

## Key Concepts

### Raskin's 5-part structure (in order)

Andy Raskin's frame, refined across hundreds of B2B narrative engagements:

1. **The shift.** Something has changed in the world — technological, regulatory, behavioural, generational — that has shifted what's possible or what's required. The shift is *outside* the company; it's something the audience experiences regardless of whether they buy.
2. **Winners and losers.** As the shift unfolds, there will be those who adapt and those who don't. The losers don't lose because they're bad people; they lose because they didn't see the shift in time or didn't respond. Stakes are clarified here.
3. **The promised land.** What does success look like in the new world? A *specific*, *desirable* future state — not "growth" or "efficiency" generically, but a concrete outcome buyers can picture themselves in.
4. **Capabilities.** What must one have or do to reach the promised land? The capabilities the buyer needs — some of which the product provides — are introduced here. The product is not the hero; the *capability* the buyer needs is the hero.
5. **Evidence.** Proof the capabilities deliver. Specific customers, specific results, specific stories. (Sharp's evidence-based discipline applied to narrative.)

**The order is load-bearing.** Most companies introduce themselves capability-first ("we make X that does Y"). That is feature pitch, not narrative. The shift-first order works because it:

- Aligns the audience to a shared frame *before* the product appears.
- Establishes stakes — "you need to do this whether you buy or not."
- Makes the product a means, not the message.

### Miller's customer-as-hero

Donald Miller (*Building a StoryBrand*): the customer is the hero of the story; the brand is the guide. Most brands invert this and make themselves the hero, which is why their narrative falls flat. The strategic narrative explicitly positions the buyer as the protagonist — *they* face the shift, *they* will win or lose, *they* will reach the promised land. The company is Obi-Wan, not Luke.

### Dunford on narrative-as-sales-pitch

April Dunford's *Sales Pitch* (2023) builds the strategic-narrative method into a B2B sales pitch structure: the narrative is the deck's opening 3-5 slides; positioning is the next 3-5; product demo follows; specific value proofs close. The same 5-part story, scaled to a 30-minute meeting.

### One narrative, many surfaces

A common failure: the company authors *separate* narratives for different audiences (investors, customers, recruits, press). The result is fragmentation — five different stories that don't reinforce. The strategic narrative is *one* core story; audience-specific adaptations are *messaging variants* of the same core, not separate narratives.

### Specificity beats abstraction

Raskin's recurring teaching: the temptation is to keep the shift abstract ("AI is changing everything"). Abstract narratives are forgettable. Specific narratives are memorable. "AI is changing everything" beats nothing; "the cost of generating production-grade software is collapsing, and the bottleneck has moved from coding to specifying" beats both.

### A narrative is not slogans

A narrative is paragraphs, not bullets. A great narrative reads naturally aloud in 90 seconds. If it reads as a list of taglines, it isn't a narrative — it's a slide.

### Test by retelling

The best validation: have someone outside the company listen to the narrative once, then try to retell it. What sticks? What gets dropped? What gets reshaped? The retelling reveals what's load-bearing vs. ornament.

## Application

### Step 1 — Confirm positioning is solid

The strategic narrative is downstream of positioning. If positioning is muddled (see [`positioning-canvas`](../positioning-canvas/SKILL.md)), the narrative will be muddled. Confirm:

- The positioning canvas exists and is approved.
- The who-it-is-for is clear.
- The unique attributes are differentiated.
- The market category is set.

If any of those is fuzzy, the narrative work pauses while positioning sharpens.

### Step 2 — Identify the shift

Brainstorm the candidate shifts. Heuristics:

- **Technological shift.** A new technology has made something newly possible (cheap compute, mobile internet, LLMs).
- **Regulatory shift.** A law / standard / requirement has changed the rules (GDPR, SOX, FedRAMP).
- **Behavioural shift.** Users / buyers behave differently now (remote work, async communication, mobile-first commerce).
- **Generational shift.** A new cohort with different expectations is moving into power (digital-native CFOs, AI-native engineers).
- **Economic shift.** Macro conditions have changed what businesses must do (capital efficiency mandates, cost-of-capital changes).
- **Competitive shift.** Incumbents have gotten weaker or new entrants have changed the field.

Pick the *one* shift that most credibly explains why your product matters *now*. The shift must be:

- **Real** — evidence supports it; reasonable people agree it's happening.
- **Specific** — not "AI is changing everything"; a specific, named change.
- **Relevant** — directly affects the buyer's world, not adjacent.
- **Unresolved** — the buyer hasn't already adapted; the response is in front of them.

### Step 3 — Identify winners and losers

For the shift, name:

- **Who adapts.** What does adaptation look like? What does the adapter's world become?
- **Who doesn't.** What happens to non-adapters? (Avoid hostile framing — losers are often sympathetic, just slow or constrained.)
- **The fork.** What's the visible inflection point between the two paths?

The stakes the narrative establishes here are what give the buyer reason to listen further.

### Step 4 — Articulate the promised land

Describe the world the buyer reaches by adapting successfully. Make it *concrete and desirable*:

- Specific outcomes (not "growth," but "monthly close in 2 days, not 10").
- Specific feelings (not "confidence," but "the CFO walks into the board meeting knowing the numbers are right").
- Specific status (not "success," but "the kind of finance function that scales-up CFOs visit to learn from").

A vague promised land produces a vague narrative.

### Step 5 — Introduce capabilities

Now the product enters. State the capabilities the buyer needs:

- Some capabilities the buyer already has.
- Some the buyer needs to acquire — possibly from the product.
- Some the buyer needs to build internally.

The product is one component among the capabilities; the narrative resists the temptation to make the product *the* capability. (If only your product can deliver, the narrative will read as marketing rather than insight.)

### Step 6 — Present evidence

Close with proof. Specific:

- **Named customer wins.** (With customer permission.) "X went from 9-day close to 2-day close in 4 months."
- **Quantified outcomes** matched to the promised land.
- **Counter-evidence acknowledged.** A narrative that pretends no counter-examples exist is suspect; acknowledging the messy ones builds trust.

### Step 7 — Draft the narrative

Write the narrative as prose, not bullets. Target 350-500 words for the full version; produce a 90-second oral version (~180 words) for executive use.

```markdown
# Strategic narrative — <Company / Product / Launch>

- **Author:** <CMO / founder>
- **Status:** Draft | Internal review | Customer-tested | Approved | In market | Stale
- **Linked positioning canvas:** <link>
- **Last updated:** <YYYY-MM-DD>

## The narrative (full version, ~350-500 words)

### 1. The shift

<2-4 sentences. State the specific shift. Establish it as real and
underway. Name what's changing about the world the buyer lives in.>

### 2. Winners and losers

<2-4 sentences. The fork. What adapters look like. What non-adapters
face. The stakes the shift imposes.>

### 3. The promised land

<3-5 sentences. The concrete, desirable future state for adapters.
Specific outcomes, specific feelings, specific status. The buyer
should picture themselves there.>

### 4. The capabilities required

<3-5 sentences. What it takes to reach the promised land. The product
enters here as one of several capabilities — never as the hero, never
as the only path.>

### 5. The evidence

<3-5 sentences. Specific proof the capabilities work. Named customers
with permission. Quantified outcomes. Honest about what's still hard.>

## The 90-second oral version (~180 words)

<Condensed version designed to read aloud naturally in 90 seconds.
Same five beats, tighter. This is the version used in keynote opens,
investor first-meetings, and the homepage hero section.>

## The 3-sentence version

<One sentence per beat or two; for use in cold-outreach openers, tagline
work, or sales-call elevator moments.>
```

### Step 8 — Validate against eight checks

1. **Order check.** Shift → winners/losers → promised land → capabilities → evidence. Not reordered. Capability is never first.
2. **Specificity check.** Each beat names specific things, not abstractions.
3. **Customer-as-hero check.** The buyer is the protagonist. The brand is the guide.
4. **Shift-realism check.** A reasonable industry observer would agree the shift is underway. The shift is not invented.
5. **Promised-land-pictureability check.** The buyer can picture themselves in the promised land. It's not abstract growth-speak.
6. **Capability-not-product check.** The fourth beat introduces capabilities the buyer needs, of which the product is one. The product is not the only option.
7. **Evidence-specificity check.** Named customers, quantified outcomes. If evidence is missing, the narrative is premature.
8. **Retell check.** Someone outside the company can listen once and retell the core beats. If they can't, the narrative isn't tight enough yet.

### Step 9 — Stress-test with stakeholders

Run the narrative past:

- **The founder / CEO.** Does the shift match their strategic frame?
- **Head of Sales.** Does the narrative make their sales conversations easier or harder?
- **3-5 customers.** Does the shift describe their world? Does the promised land match what they want?
- **3-5 prospects who said no.** Does the narrative reframe why they should reconsider?
- **A skeptical board member.** Where does it sound hollow?

Reactions are evidence. Specifically, prospects-who-said-no are the most diagnostic audience — their reactions reveal whether the narrative is robust against scrutiny.

### Step 10 — Cascade into surfaces

Once approved, the narrative becomes the input to:

- **Investor pitch deck.** Opening 3-5 slides are the narrative.
- **Sales pitch.** Per Dunford's *Sales Pitch*, opening 3-5 slides are the narrative.
- **Keynote / all-hands.** The 90-second version opens the talk.
- **Home page.** Hero section is the 3-sentence version; longer-form scroll is the full version.
- **Recruiting page.** The mission paragraph derives from the narrative's promised land + capabilities.

Drift between the narrative and downstream surfaces is the CMO's accountability. Each surface references the narrative explicitly.

### Step 11 — Refresh on triggers

Narratives age. Refresh triggers:

- **Shift maturation.** The shift becomes obvious; "we saw it first" stops being meaningful. The narrative may need to evolve from "this shift is coming" to "this shift has happened; now what."
- **New shift.** A second shift overlays the first; narrative must integrate.
- **Customer evidence saturation.** The original evidence is dated; refresh with current customers.
- **Annual minimum** for high-stakes companies.

## Examples

### Example 1 — A sharp narrative (good, sketch)

**Full version** (for LedgerStack, the multi-entity accounting product from the positioning-canvas example):

> Over the past five years, B2B software has trained companies to expect that revenue could be earned on day one. SaaS pricing, usage-based billing, embedded finance, and AI co-pilots have made "ship fast, charge fast" the default mode. But the *finance function* hasn't kept up. While the rest of the business operates in real time, finance still closes the books in two-week sprints, runs intercompany on spreadsheets, and reports last month's results halfway through this one. The gap between operating speed and reporting speed is now the largest hidden tax on scale-up CFOs. **(The shift.)**
>
> CFOs who close this gap are the ones who get to run the company. They walk into board meetings knowing the numbers; they answer investor questions without phoning Finance; they restructure when restructuring is cheap, not when it's forced. CFOs who don't close the gap spend their week in Excel, lose strategic credibility, and watch their CEO hire a controller for the next round. **(Winners and losers.)**
>
> The promised land is a Finance function that operates at the speed of the business it serves. Month-end is 2 days, not 10. The CEO checks real-time consolidated P&L without asking. Adding a new entity for an acquisition takes 15 minutes, not a quarter. The audit takes a week of preparation, not a month. Finance is what scale-ups visit to learn from, not what they hire McKinsey to fix. **(Promised land.)**
>
> Reaching it requires four capabilities most multi-entity SMB Finance teams don't have today: a native multi-entity ledger (not consolidated spreadsheets), real-time consolidated reporting (not batch), automated intercompany elimination (not manual), and an API that lets engineering build internal tools on the ledger (not a CSV export). Some of these capabilities you build in-house; some you buy. LedgerStack provides the ledger and the consolidation; you provide the team and the process discipline. **(Capabilities.)**
>
> Companies that have made this transition: a 7-entity logistics scale-up that took month-end from 9 days to 2 in four months. A 12-entity media holding company whose CFO walked into Series C diligence with a 24-hour close. A 4-entity B2B SaaS that did its first audit in three weeks instead of an estimated five. Not magic — disciplined Finance teams who stopped fighting their stack and started using one built for them. **(Evidence.)**

A reader can argue with the shift (is the gap really the largest tax?), the alternatives (is "spreadsheets" really the default?), the evidence (are those customers reachable for reference calls?) — every claim is falsifiable. Which is the property the skill exists to enforce.

### Example 2 — Failure mode (bad)

> LedgerStack is the leading platform for modern accounting. We help finance teams close faster with AI-powered consolidation and real-time reporting. Built by experts in finance technology, we serve hundreds of customers across multiple industries. Join the future of finance.

Failures: capability-first ("LedgerStack is the leading platform..."); no shift; no winners/losers; no promised land; "AI-powered" applied generically; "hundreds of customers" is the laziest evidence shape; brand is the hero. A reader retains nothing.

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1 — Capability-first opening

**Symptom:** "We are X that does Y for Z."

**Consequence:** Reader has no frame for why the product matters now. Memorability collapses. The opening reads as one of dozens of similar pitches.

**Fix:** Lead with the shift. Make the buyer experience the world as having changed before the product is mentioned.

### Pitfall 2 — Abstract shift

**Symptom:** "AI is changing everything." "Digital transformation is here."

**Consequence:** Forgettable. The buyer has heard this dozens of times; nothing about the specific narrative sticks.

**Fix:** Name a specific shift. "The cost of generating production-grade software is collapsing, and the bottleneck has moved from coding to specifying" is specific; "AI is changing everything" is not.

### Pitfall 3 — Brand-as-hero

**Symptom:** Every section opens with "we" or the company name. The buyer is mentioned but as a beneficiary, not a protagonist.

**Consequence:** Miller's diagnosis: brand-as-hero copy doesn't convert. Buyers tune out.

**Fix:** Customer-as-hero. The buyer faces the shift; the buyer reaches the promised land; the buyer needs the capabilities. The brand is the guide that makes the hero's path possible.

### Pitfall 4 — Hand-waved promised land

**Symptom:** "Reach your potential." "Unlock growth." "Move faster."

**Consequence:** The buyer can't picture the destination. Without pictureable destination, no narrative momentum.

**Fix:** Concrete, specific, sensory. "Month-end in 2 days, not 10" is pictureable; "operate efficiently" is not.

### Pitfall 5 — Capability-as-only-path

**Symptom:** The fourth beat implies the buyer can reach the promised land *only* through the product.

**Consequence:** Reads as marketing rather than insight. Buyers become skeptical because the framing is too convenient.

**Fix:** The product is one of several capabilities. Some the buyer brings; some they build; some they buy from elsewhere. This builds trust and makes the product's role legible.

### Pitfall 6 — Evidence-free narrative

**Symptom:** The narrative is internally elegant but the evidence section is missing or generic ("hundreds of happy customers").

**Consequence:** A polished narrative without evidence reads as fiction; sophisticated buyers discount accordingly.

**Fix:** Named customers, specific outcomes, with permission. If you don't have evidence, the narrative isn't ready — keep gathering.

### Pitfall 7 — Five different narratives for five audiences

**Symptom:** The company has separate narratives for investors, customers, recruits, press.

**Consequence:** Fragmentation. The investor hears one story; the customer hears another; the recruit hears a third; none of them reinforce.

**Fix:** One core narrative; audience-specific *messaging* derives from it. Investors get the same five beats with different emphasis on TAM and traction; customers get the same five beats with different emphasis on workflow.

### Pitfall 8 — Narrative as slide

**Symptom:** The "narrative" is a slide with five bullet points.

**Consequence:** It's not a narrative. Narratives are paragraphs that read aloud. Slides are summaries of narratives, not substitutes.

**Fix:** Write prose. Read it aloud. Time it. Iterate until the 90-second version is natural.

### Pitfall 9 — One-and-done

**Symptom:** Narrative was written at Series A; the company is now Series C with a different category and customer base.

**Consequence:** The shift the narrative names is over. New customers don't recognize themselves. The narrative dates the company.

**Fix:** Refresh triggers. Annual minimum. The narrative is a living strategic artifact, not a brochure.
