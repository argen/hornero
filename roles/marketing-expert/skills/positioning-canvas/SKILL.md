# Positioning canvas

> v0 draft. Grounded in April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome: How to Nail Product Positioning So Customers Get It, Buy It, Love It* (2019) and *Sales Pitch* (2023), Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* (1991, rev. 2014), Theodore Levitt's "Marketing Myopia" (1960), and Mark Ritson's strategic-marketing essays.

## Purpose

Produce a positioning canvas that:

1. **Names the competitive alternatives** specifically (including "do nothing" and "do it in a spreadsheet" — Dunford's most under-appreciated insight).
2. **Names the unique attributes** the product has that the alternatives don't.
3. **Translates those attributes into value** for the buyer — the outcome the buyer is trying to achieve.
4. **Names the segment that values this most** — the "who cares most" segment that becomes the beachhead.
5. **States the market category** — the frame buyers use to make sense of the product when they first encounter it.

The canvas is the load-bearing artifact for every downstream marketing decision. Without it, narrative drifts, copy generalises, channels are picked by fashion, pricing is set by gut. With it, each downstream decision has a referent.

A positioning canvas that any competitor could swap names into and have still read is *not positioning* — it is a description of the category. The skill exists to make the difference unambiguous.

## Key Concepts

### Dunford's five components

April Dunford (*Obviously Awesome*) reframes positioning as five interlocking components, in order:

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What would buyers use to solve this problem if your product didn't exist? Not just "other software in this category" — also manual processes, status-quo workarounds, and "live with the problem." A buyer who would otherwise use spreadsheets faces a very different positioning than one who would use Salesforce.
2. **Unique attributes.** The features, capabilities, integrations, or assets your product has that the named alternatives lack. Stated specifically — not "easy to use," but "configurable in 15 minutes by a non-technical admin where Salesforce takes a 6-week implementation."
3. **Value (and who-cares-most).** For each unique attribute, the outcome it produces for the buyer. *Then* the segment for whom that outcome matters most. The segment that "cares most" is the segment where the positioning resonates without friction.
4. **Market category.** The mental frame buyers use to encounter the product. The wrong frame leads to wrong-expectation mismatches; the right frame triggers the right comparison set in the buyer's head.
5. **Who-it-is-for.** The specific buyer the positioning targets. Job title, company stage, current pain. Sharp enough that adjacent segments can self-identify as out of scope.

### Levitt's marketing myopia

Theodore Levitt (1960, *Harvard Business Review*): companies fail by defining themselves by what they sell rather than what customers buy. Railroads thought they were in the railroad business; they were in the transportation business. The positioning canvas resists this by anchoring on the *job the customer is trying to do* (Christensen's JTBD; Dunford translates this into the canvas's "value" component) rather than on the product's features.

### Moore on categories

Geoffrey Moore (*Crossing the Chasm*): the market category is a buyer's shortcut. Buyers don't have time to evaluate every product on first principles; they reach for the category that frames their expectation. Positioning a product in the wrong category invites wrong comparisons and wrong objections.

The positioning canvas states the category explicitly — sometimes the category is established (CRM, project management), sometimes the product is creating a new category (Drift's "conversational marketing"). New-category positioning is a *strategic choice with long lead times*; established-category positioning is a *tactical choice with faster feedback*.

### "Who cares most" segmentation

Dunford's sharpest contribution: instead of trying to sell to "everyone with the problem," the positioning identifies the segment for whom the unique attributes deliver disproportionate value. Two reasons:

- **Targeting resource discipline.** Marketing budgets are finite. Concentrating against the who-cares-most segment beats diffusing across the addressable market.
- **Reference customers compound.** The who-cares-most segment produces reference customers whose word-of-mouth lands within their segment, accelerating adoption among the people positioning is meant to reach.

Adjacent segments aren't ignored — they self-qualify once the who-cares-most segment has produced visible traction.

### Sharp enough that some buyers opt out

Mark Ritson, Dunford, and the wider strategic-marketing canon converge on one test: positioning is sharp when *some* buyers, upon reading it, decide it's not for them. Generic positioning ("we help businesses grow with AI-powered insights") never triggers opt-out; it never triggers opt-in either. The buyer who would have been ideal scrolls past because nothing said "this is for you, specifically."

Positioning's strategic claim: by deliberately repelling the wrong buyers, you attract the right ones harder.

### Positioning vs messaging vs copy

The canvas is *positioning* — the strategic answer to "what is this and who is it for." It is not:

- **Messaging.** How positioning is communicated to a specific audience. The same positioning produces different messaging for different audiences (technical buyer vs economic buyer; consumer vs enterprise).
- **Copy.** The specific words on a specific surface (homepage, ad, sales deck). Copy implements messaging; messaging implements positioning.

Skipping levels — writing copy without messaging, or messaging without positioning — produces the "every campaign feels different" symptom and the "we keep rewriting the homepage" symptom.

### Evidence-based, not opinion-driven

The canvas is grounded in customer evidence — interviews, sales-call recordings, win-loss analysis, support tickets, churned-customer interviews. Positioning written in a marketing-leadership offsite without customer evidence is *internal positioning* — what the team thinks the product is — which routinely fails to map to what the market actually responds to.

## Application

### Step 1 — Gather evidence (the slow part)

Before drafting, the CMO / head of marketing assembles:

- **15-30 customer interviews** drawn from current customers, recent wins, recent losses, and (where reachable) prospects who chose an alternative. Focus questions: "What were you using before? Why did you start looking? What were you comparing us to? What almost stopped you from buying?"
- **Sales-call recordings** — listen for buyer language. Note the words buyers use to describe the problem, not the words the seller uses.
- **Win-loss analysis** — for the last N deals, what was the deciding factor? Patterns surface — the deciding factor is rarely the feature the team thinks it is.
- **Competitive review** — what does each named alternative actually do? Test their product; read their docs; not just their marketing.
- **Internal interviews** — sales reps, customer success, founders. Where do they hear buyers express their problem in unfiltered terms?

If this evidence is missing, the first phase is gathering it. Drafting positioning without evidence produces positioning the market doesn't recognise.

### Step 2 — Draft the canvas

```markdown
# Positioning canvas — <Product / line>

- **Author:** <CMO / head of marketing>
- **Status:** Draft | Validating | Approved | In market | Stale
- **Last updated:** <YYYY-MM-DD>
- **Evidence basis:** <N customer interviews; M sales-call hours; period covered>

## 1. Competitive alternatives

For each alternative, state what buyers actually use it for (not what the
competitor markets it as), and why it falls short for the buyer we're
targeting.

- **Alternative A — <name>.** What it is for our target buyer; why it falls short.
- **Alternative B — <name>.** …
- **Alternative C — Do it in spreadsheets / manually.** What this looks like; why it persists; what triggers buyers to outgrow it.
- **Alternative D — Do nothing / live with the problem.** When this is the realistic default and why.

(Most positioning misses by under-counting the spreadsheet and do-nothing
alternatives. They are not "no competition" — they are the dominant
competitor for most B2B products in their first years.)

## 2. Unique attributes

What does our product have that none of the alternatives have? Stated
specifically, not aspirationally. A feature is unique only if no named
alternative does it.

- **Unique attribute 1.** <Specific capability. Stated as "we do X" or "we
  have Y," not "we are easy" or "we are fast.">
- **Unique attribute 2.** …
- **Unique attribute 3.** …

(Aim for 3-7. Fewer than 3 is a sign the positioning isn't differentiated
enough to need this canvas. More than 7 means you're listing features,
not attributes that matter.)

## 3. Value (and who-cares-most)

For each unique attribute, name the *outcome* it produces for the buyer.
Then name the segment for whom that outcome matters most.

| Unique attribute | Value (buyer outcome) | Who cares most |
|---|---|---|
| <attribute> | <outcome — what improves in the buyer's world> | <specific segment by role, size, context> |

The who-cares-most segment that recurs across most rows is the beachhead.
If the rows produce different segments, either the positioning is fractured
or the product is two products.

## 4. Market category

What category do buyers reach for when they encounter this product? Three
choices:

- **Established category.** <Name a known category, e.g., "CRM,"
  "project management," "observability." Then: why this category and
  not adjacent ones?>
- **Adjacent / niche category.** <A narrower or more specific category
  within an established one, e.g., "CRM for multi-entity B2B
  businesses">
- **New category creation.** <A category we are deliberately defining.
  Long lead time; only justified by genuine market-shape change. State
  why creating the category beats positioning in an existing one.>

Note: most products should pick established or adjacent. New-category
creation is a multi-year strategic commitment; teams routinely
under-estimate the cost.

## 5. Who it is for

State the specific buyer. Sharp enough that someone reading it can
self-identify in or out.

- **Job title / role:** <e.g., "Head of Finance at a multi-entity SMB,"
  not "decision-maker.">
- **Company stage / size:** <e.g., "Series A-B, 50-200 employees, 2-15
  legal entities," not "growing companies.">
- **Current pain:** <One sentence describing the pain in the buyer's own
  language — drawn from interview evidence, not invented.>
- **Trigger to look:** <What event prompts this buyer to start looking?
  Hiring a new CFO; closing a Series B; failing an audit; spreadsheet
  errors costing them money.>

## Out of scope — who this is NOT for

State 1-3 adjacent segments who might think this is for them but
shouldn't. Pre-emptive opt-out saves both sides time.

- <Segment that might come in thinking we're for them; why we're not.>
- <…>

## Anti-positioning self-check

Could a named competitor swap their name into this canvas and have it
still read? If yes, sharpen until no.
```

### Step 3 — Validate against six checks

1. **Specificity check.** A reader can name the target buyer in one sentence after reading the canvas.
2. **Alternatives-include-status-quo check.** "Do nothing" and "do it in spreadsheets" appear, and are not dismissed.
3. **Unique attributes check.** Each attribute is genuinely unique among the named alternatives — verified by checking competitor sites and trying their products.
4. **Who-cares-most coherence check.** The recurring segment in the value table matches the who-it-is-for component. If they diverge, positioning is fractured.
5. **Category-cost check.** If positioning in a new category, has the multi-year cost been acknowledged? Most should be in an established or adjacent category.
6. **Opt-out check.** Some reasonable buyers, upon reading this, decide it's not for them. If everyone the team can think of self-identifies as a fit, the positioning is too broad.

### Step 4 — Validate against customers

The canvas is *drafted internally*, *validated externally*. After draft, run it past:

- 5-10 customers in the who-cares-most segment. Does it match their experience?
- 5-10 prospects in the same segment. When they hear it, does it land?
- 3-5 sales reps. Does it match what they say in sales calls?
- 1-2 advisory customers or board members with category expertise.

Reactions are evidence. "I think it's great" is not as useful as "I'd be skeptical of the 'multi-entity' framing because we never thought of ourselves that way until you put words on it."

### Step 5 — Cascade into downstream artifacts

Once approved, the canvas becomes the input to:

- **Strategic narrative** (`strategic-narrative` skill) — the 5-part story for keynotes / decks / all-hands.
- **Launch brief** (`launch-brief` skill) — the channel + audience + sequence plan.
- **Messaging frameworks** — audience-specific translations of the positioning.
- **Copy across every surface** — homepage, sales decks, ads, onboarding emails.
- **Pricing and packaging decisions** — the segment's willingness-to-pay is anchored to the value the canvas surfaces.

Drift between the canvas and downstream artifacts is a maintenance failure, not a fact of life. The CMO owns alignment.

### Step 6 — Refresh on triggers

Positioning is not a one-time exercise. Refresh triggers:

- **Competitive entry / exit.** A new alternative changes the canvas's first component; cascade through.
- **Segment shift.** The who-cares-most segment may shift as the product matures (Moore's chasm-crossing).
- **Win-loss data drift.** When the deciding factor in deals changes, positioning may need to follow.
- **Pricing change.** Significant pricing changes often require positioning to shift toward the segments that can absorb the new pricing.
- **Annual minimum.** Even without triggers, a CMO refreshes the canvas annually against current evidence.

## Examples

### Example 1 — Useful positioning canvas (good, sketch)

```markdown
# Positioning canvas — LedgerStack (multi-entity accounting platform)

Status: Approved, in market since 2025-Q2.
Evidence: 22 customer interviews, 60 hours sales calls, 18-month
win-loss analysis (deals n=47).

## 1. Competitive alternatives
- Alternative A — NetSuite: enterprise ERP. Buyers use it because it
  supports multi-entity, but it costs $50-200k+/year and a 4-9 month
  implementation. Falls short for SMB buyers: cost, complexity, time-
  to-value, ongoing admin burden.
- Alternative B — QuickBooks Online Advanced + manual consolidation:
  buyers stitch multiple QBO files and consolidate quarterly in
  spreadsheets. Falls short: month-end takes 5-10 days; intercompany
  is manual; audit prep is brutal; CFO can't see real-time consolidated
  P&L.
- Alternative C — Do it in spreadsheets entirely: 30% of our pre-sales
  pipeline. Persists because it works at very small scale (2 entities,
  <$5M revenue). Triggers to outgrow: adding the 3rd entity; the first
  audit; the first CFO hire who refuses to work this way.
- Alternative D — Do nothing / live with the problem: more common than
  it should be. The CEO knows it's painful but the pain hasn't crossed
  the threshold. Trigger: Series B board meeting where the board asks
  about consolidated reporting.

## 2. Unique attributes
- Native multi-entity ledger: every transaction lives at the entity
  level; consolidation is a query, not a process. NetSuite has this;
  QBO does not.
- 15-minute admin setup for a new entity. NetSuite requires consulting.
- API-first architecture: ERP-grade data accessible via REST + GraphQL.
  Neither NetSuite nor QBO offers this for SMB pricing.
- Built-in intercompany elimination at close. Most SMB buyers do this
  manually in spreadsheets; NetSuite has it; QBO does not.
- Real-time consolidated dashboard. NetSuite has consolidated reporting
  but it's batch; ours is real-time. QBO doesn't.

## 3. Value table
| Unique attribute | Value | Who cares most |
|---|---|---|
| Native multi-entity ledger | Month-end goes from 5-10 days to 1-2 | Head of Finance / CFO at 2-15 entity B2B |
| 15-min entity setup | Acquisitions and new-market launches don't take a quarter | CFOs of acquisitive companies |
| API-first | Engineering can build internal tools on the ledger | Finance-engineering teams at scale-ups |
| Intercompany elimination | Audit prep goes from weeks to days | CFOs prepping for Series B / audit |
| Real-time dashboard | CEO sees consolidated P&L without asking Finance | CEO + CFO at multi-entity scale-ups |

Recurring segment: Head of Finance / CFO at multi-entity B2B
scale-ups (Series A-C, 2-15 entities). Beachhead confirmed.

## 4. Market category
- Choice: Adjacent. We are positioned as "Accounting for multi-entity
  B2B," explicitly narrower than "ERP" and explicitly more specialised
  than "QBO + spreadsheets."
- Why not new-category creation: "Multi-entity accounting" is a known
  concept; we don't need to teach the category, we need to teach that
  it's affordable below NetSuite. Multi-year category creation is
  unjustified given the alternative is faster.

## 5. Who it is for
- Job title: Head of Finance or CFO. (Buyer; CEO is often the champion;
  Finance team operates the product daily.)
- Company stage: Series A-C, 50-300 employees, 2-15 legal entities.
- Current pain (buyer's language, drawn from interviews): "Month-end
  takes a week because I'm stitching files." "I can't get a real P&L
  before the 12th." "Audit nearly killed me last year."
- Trigger to look: hiring a new CFO; closing a Series B; failing an
  audit; the 3rd-entity threshold.

## Out of scope
- Single-entity SMBs <$5M revenue: QBO is right for them.
- Enterprise multi-entity (>15 entities, >$200M revenue): NetSuite has
  more advanced consolidation features that justify its cost.
- Non-profits: regulatory shape is different; out of scope until v3.

## Anti-positioning self-check
Could NetSuite or QBO swap their name in? No — NetSuite's enterprise
pricing makes "15-minute setup" structurally impossible; QBO doesn't
have native multi-entity. The canvas reads only for us.
```

A reviewer can argue with the segment definition, the "15-minute" claim, or the "real-time" claim — they can verify each against evidence. The positioning is falsifiable, which is the point.

### Example 2 — Failure mode (bad)

```markdown
# Positioning canvas
- We help businesses grow with AI-powered insights.
- Our unique attribute is that we're really easy to use.
- It's for everyone who needs better data.
```

Failures: no named alternatives; no specific attribute (every product claims "easy to use"); no segment; no category; "everyone who needs better data" is not positioning. A buyer cannot self-identify; a competitor could swap names verbatim.

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1 — Positioning by committee

**Symptom:** Multiple stakeholders contribute language; the result is generic, hedging, true of every competitor.

**Consequence:** Marketing budget amplifies the lack of clarity. Sales cycles lengthen because buyers can't tell what the product is.

**Fix:** One owner (CMO / head of marketing); committee input but not committee writing. Validate against customers, not against internal stakeholders.

### Pitfall 2 — Alternatives that are only "other software in the category"

**Symptom:** The alternatives section lists 3 competitors; nothing about spreadsheets, manual processes, or do-nothing.

**Consequence:** The largest competitor (status quo) is invisible to the positioning. The product's positioning doesn't engage with the buyer's actual decision.

**Fix:** Status-quo alternatives (manual, spreadsheets, do-nothing) are mandatory rows. For most B2B products in their first years, these are the dominant competitor.

### Pitfall 3 — "Easy to use" as unique attribute

**Symptom:** Unique attributes include "easy to use," "fast," "intuitive," "modern."

**Consequence:** These are claimed by every competitor; they are not unique. The positioning relies on a differentiator the buyer doesn't experience as distinctive.

**Fix:** State unique attributes specifically. "15-minute admin setup by a non-technical user" is specific; "easy to use" is not. If you can't state it specifically, it isn't actually unique.

### Pitfall 4 — Targeting everyone with the problem

**Symptom:** "Who it's for" reads as "any business with X pain."

**Consequence:** Marketing budget diffuses; messaging defaults to the most general possible audience; nobody self-identifies as a clear fit.

**Fix:** Dunford's who-cares-most. Concentrate against the segment for whom the unique attributes deliver disproportionate value. The rest will self-qualify later.

### Pitfall 5 — New-category creation without justification

**Symptom:** The team chooses to define a new category because it sounds strategic.

**Consequence:** Multi-year category-creation cost incurred; the company doesn't have the patience or budget; halfway through, they revert to positioning in an existing category but with confusion already sown.

**Fix:** New-category creation is justified only when the market shape has genuinely changed and no existing category accommodates the product. Default to established or adjacent.

### Pitfall 6 — Evidence-free positioning

**Symptom:** The canvas is written in an off-site without customer interview data.

**Consequence:** Internal positioning ("what we think we are") doesn't match external positioning ("what the market responds to"). Months of campaigns fail to land before the team realises why.

**Fix:** Evidence basis stated explicitly in the canvas's header (N interviews, M hours of sales calls). Positioning is drafted internally but anchored externally.

### Pitfall 7 — Canvas filed, never used

**Symptom:** Canvas is produced; downstream artifacts (narrative, copy, channel plans) don't reference it.

**Consequence:** Drift. Every campaign feels different. Sales reps tell a different story than marketing. The canvas's authority erodes.

**Fix:** The CMO enforces cascade: every downstream artifact references the canvas. When the canvas updates, downstream artifacts update.

### Pitfall 8 — No refresh

**Symptom:** Canvas was produced at launch; never updated despite a new competitor, a segment shift, and a pricing change.

**Consequence:** Positioning ages; the market moves; the team optimises against a stale frame.

**Fix:** Refresh triggers explicit (competitive entry, segment shift, win-loss drift, pricing change, annual minimum). The canvas is a living document.
