# VOC mining

> v0 draft. Grounded in Joanna Wiebe's voice-of-customer copy methodology (Copy Hackers), Bob Moesta's demand-side switch interview (JTBD-applied-to-marketing), April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome* (2019) on positioning grounded in customer evidence, Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) on Category Entry Points, and Marty Cagan's *Inspired* on value risk as the foundational product risk.

## Purpose

Produce a voice-of-customer mining plan that:

1. **Names the corpora** — where real customer language lives, and which corpora are richest for which questions.
2. **States the extraction questions** — what specifically to look for in each corpus.
3. **Specifies the synthesis method** — how raw quotes become actionable copy patterns, awareness-level signals, and category-entry-point evidence.
4. **Distinguishes marketing mining from PM mining** — the corpora are often the same; the questions and outputs are different.

VOC mining is not research for its own sake. It is research in service of a specific output: copy that converts because it uses the buyer's language, positioning that lands because it matches the buyer's frame, and channel strategy that reaches buyers in the moments they think about the category.

The discipline separates two failure modes: (1) copy written entirely from the inside of the company, using vocabulary that the building team invented and the buying audience doesn't recognize; (2) copy that paraphrases research findings rather than using the exact customer phrases that do the converting.

## Key Concepts

### Wiebe: the best copy already exists

Joanna Wiebe's foundational claim: before any copywriter types a word, they should mine what customers have already said. The exact phrases customers use to describe their problem, their previous solution, and the value they wanted are more persuasive than anything a copywriter invents — because they trigger recognition rather than persuasion.

**Wiebe's core corpora:**

1. **App store / marketplace reviews** (G2, Capterra, Trustpilot, App Store). High-signal for: reasons for switching, specific pain points, specific outcomes achieved, language used to describe the category.
2. **Competitor reviews.** The pain points described in 1-2-star competitor reviews are the pain your product must claim to solve. The praise in 4-5-star competitor reviews is the baseline expectation your product must meet.
3. **Support tickets / helpdesk transcripts.** High-signal for: friction in the current experience, problems that recur (recurrence = severity), language used when buyers are frustrated or confused.
4. **Sales call transcripts.** High-signal for: questions buyers ask before converting (these are objections and doubts your copy must address), language used to describe the trigger that prompted them to look, how buyers describe the competitor they're coming from.
5. **Reddit / Quora / community threads.** High-signal for: unfiltered problem language (buyers describe their problem to peers, not to vendors, so the language is unguarded), category language (what buyers call the category when talking to each other), and consideration criteria (what dimensions buyers say they care about).
6. **Churn-exit interviews.** High-signal for: where the product failed to deliver value, alternative solutions the buyer returned to or switched to, language used to describe unmet expectations.

### Moesta: the switch interview as corpus

Bob Moesta's demand-side JTBD methodology produces a specific interview corpus — the switch interview — that is different from standard customer-discovery interviews:

The switch interview traces the **timeline from first thought to first use**: what was happening in the buyer's life before they started looking; what triggered the first search; what they looked at and rejected; what tipped the decision; what was happening in the first weeks of use. The interview is not about features or satisfaction; it is about the *causality of the switch*.

For marketing mining, this corpus yields:
- **The trigger moment** — the specific event that prompted the buyer to start looking. This is the Category Entry Point (Sharp) and often the highest-signal input for copy.
- **The language used to describe the switch** — often strikingly different from the language marketing uses.
- **The anxiety at the decision** — what the buyer was afraid of when they bought. Copy that addresses this anxiety before the buyer voices it converts.
- **The progress the buyer was trying to make** — the functional job (what they needed to accomplish), the emotional job (how they wanted to feel), and the social job (how they wanted to be seen).

### Dunford: VOC feeds the positioning canvas

April Dunford (*Obviously Awesome*): the positioning canvas is built on customer evidence, not internal consensus. VOC mining is the evidence-gathering phase that makes positioning possible.

Specific inputs from VOC to the positioning canvas:

- **Competitive alternatives** — what buyers say they were using before or would use if the product didn't exist (found in switch interviews and app reviews).
- **Unique attributes** — what buyers say was different or better (found in positive reviews and sales calls), verified against competitor reviews to confirm uniqueness.
- **Value (who-cares-most)** — the outcomes buyers describe as the reason they stayed (found in positive reviews and success-story interviews), plus the segment patterns that emerge from those.
- **Category** — what vocabulary buyers use when describing the product to peers (found in community threads and referral conversations).

### Sharp: Category Entry Points from VOC

Byron Sharp (*How Brands Grow*): brands grow by being thought of in **Category Entry Points** — the situations, triggers, and contexts in which buyers think about the category. Mental availability is built by consistently linking the brand to those entry points.

VOC mining surfaces the entry points: buyers say what was happening when they first searched, what prompted them to look, what they Googled, what they described to the colleague who referred them. The patterns across these descriptions are the Category Entry Points marketing must own.

Example: if 60% of switch-interview subjects say some version of "we had just hired a new CFO and she said she couldn't work with what we had," the Category Entry Point is "new CFO hire." Marketing investments that reach buyers at the new-CFO-hire moment are more valuable than those that reach the same buyers at other times.

### Cagan: VOC as value-risk evidence

Marty Cagan (*Inspired*): the four product risks are value (will customers buy?), usability (can they use it?), feasibility (can we build it?), and viability (can the business support it?). VOC mining is evidence for value risk — the most important risk to resolve first.

Marketing's mining differs from PM's mining in its output question: **PM mines to understand "does this problem exist and is it urgent enough to solve?"**; marketing mines to understand **"in what words should we describe this problem so buyers recognize themselves?"**

The corpora overlap almost entirely. The difference is the output artifact:

- PM produces: problem statement, user stories, success criteria, feature prioritization.
- Marketing produces: copy patterns, messaging framework, awareness-level map, Category Entry Point list.

When both roles mine the same corpus independently, they surface complementary evidence. The copy pattern mining is not a duplication of PM's discovery work; it is a parallel extraction from the same material.

### The language-as-data principle

The specific words customers use are not just evidence of a problem — they *are* the copy. Wiebe's method:

1. Collect exact quotes (not paraphrases).
2. Note frequency: phrases that appear multiple times are load-bearing.
3. Note context: the same phrase in a review and in a sales call and in a support ticket is as close to ground truth as VOC mining gets.
4. Note surprise: phrases that marketing would never have written but that customers keep using are the highest-signal findings. The surprise is the signal.

## Application

### Step 1 — Define the mining brief

Before touching any corpus, state:

- **What copy will this mining inform?** (Homepage rewrite? Specific email? Sales deck? Positioning refresh?)
- **Who is the segment?** (ICP from positioning canvas, or the segment whose language you're mining.)
- **What awareness level do you believe they are at?** (Schwartz's five levels — your inference; VOC will confirm or correct.)
- **What is the primary unknown?** (What problem language do they use? What triggers them to look? What objections must copy address? What Category Entry Points exist?)

A mining brief that answers these questions produces useful output. Mining without a brief produces a large corpus and no synthesis.

### Step 2 — Select and prioritize corpora

Not all corpora are equally accessible or equally rich for every question. Prioritize:

| Corpus | Richest for | Typical access | Volume needed |
|--------|-------------|----------------|---------------|
| G2 / Capterra / app reviews (product + competitors) | Problem language, switching triggers, outcome language | Public | 50-200 reviews |
| Sales call transcripts | Trigger language, objections, competitor comparisons | Internal (Gong, Chorus, notes) | 20-40 calls |
| Support tickets / helpdesk | Friction, recurring problems, confusion language | Internal (Zendesk, Intercom) | 50-100 tickets |
| Reddit / community threads | Unfiltered category language, consideration criteria, peer recommendations | Public | 30-80 threads |
| Switch interviews (Moesta) | Trigger timeline, anxiety at decision, progress language | Primary research | 8-15 interviews |
| Churn-exit interviews | Failed expectations, competitor returned to, exit language | Internal | 10-20 interviews |
| NPS survey verbatims | High-trust language from promoters; friction language from detractors | Internal | All verbatims from last 12 months |

If time and access are limited: **reviews + sales call transcripts** cover 70% of the signal for most B2B products. Start there.

### Step 3 — Extract by question type

For each corpus, apply extraction questions:

**From reviews (product and competitor):**
- What specific problem does the reviewer name? (Exact quote.)
- What were they using before? (Signals competitive alternative.)
- What outcome did they achieve? (Exact quote — this becomes benefit copy.)
- What almost stopped them from buying or using? (Signals objection copy.)
- What words do they use to describe the category? (Signals category language.)

**From sales call transcripts:**
- What event prompted the buyer to start looking? (First appearance of a Category Entry Point — exact words used.)
- What were they comparing to? (Exact alternatives named.)
- What question do they ask most before buying? (This is the objection the copy must pre-empt.)
- What outcome do they describe hoping to achieve? (Exact future-state language — this is the promised-land copy.)

**From support tickets:**
- What problems recur most? (Frequency = severity — also signals where copy may have over-promised.)
- What language do buyers use when confused? (If the confusion is category-level, copy may need to address it earlier.)
- What do buyers say they expected vs. what they got? (Signals copy-to-product expectation gap.)

**From Reddit/community:**
- How do buyers describe the category to peers? (No vendor vocabulary contamination — this is how buyers talk among themselves.)
- What criteria do they say they evaluate on? (These are the questions the copy must answer.)
- What do they recommend, and in what words? (Referral language is the highest-trust copy signal.)

**From switch interviews (Moesta):**
- What was happening in the buyer's life 3-6 months before they started looking? (Background context.)
- What was the specific trigger event? (Exact description — primary Category Entry Point.)
- What did they Google first? (Exact language — SEO and copy headline signal.)
- What were they afraid of when they bought? (Exact anxiety — this must be addressed in copy before the buyer voices it.)

### Step 4 — Synthesise into output artifacts

Raw quotes must become usable outputs. Four synthesis artifacts:

**Artifact 1 — Copy pattern library**

A table of high-frequency, high-signal quotes organized by copy function:

```markdown
| Copy function | Customer quote | Source | Frequency |
|---------------|---------------|--------|-----------|
| Problem statement | "Every month-end I'm stitching files for 10 days" | G2 reviews (n=12) | High |
| Trigger | "We hired a new CFO and she said she couldn't work like this" | Sales calls (n=7) | High |
| Promise / outcome | "Now the board meeting starts with good numbers, not with me apologizing" | Switch interview (n=4) | Moderate |
| Objection | "I was worried it would take months to set up like NetSuite" | Sales calls (n=9) | High |
| Category language | "multi-entity accounting" / "consolidated reporting" | Reddit (n=15) | High |
```

High-frequency quotes in Problem statement and Trigger are the headline candidates. High-frequency quotes in Objection are the copy that must appear *before* the CTA.

**Artifact 2 — Awareness-level map**

State, with evidence:
- The majority of buyers arrive at this surface at awareness level: [X].
- Evidence: [specific quote patterns that confirm the level].
- Implication for copy opening: [lead with problem / shift / differentiation / offer].

**Artifact 3 — Category Entry Points list**

The top 3-5 situations, triggers, and contexts in which buyers first thought about the category. Each entry point is:
- Named (e.g., "new CFO hire").
- Evidenced (frequency across corpora).
- Linked to a copy and/or channel implication.

**Artifact 4 — Company-language / customer-language contrast**

A table contrasting:
- The words the internal team uses (from existing marketing copy, sales decks, job descriptions).
- The words customers use for the same concept (from VOC corpus).

This contrast directly informs the copy critique process and the homepage rewrite brief.

### Step 5 — Hand off to copy, positioning, and channel

The output artifacts feed downstream:
- **Copy**: copy pattern library → headline candidates, body-copy structures, objection-addressing sequences.
- **Positioning**: copy pattern library + Category Entry Points → competitive-alternatives and value sections of the positioning canvas.
- **Channel**: Category Entry Points → moments and contexts where buyers are reachable; informs channel-fit evaluation (when to be there).

## Examples

### Example 1 — Mining plan for a homepage rewrite (B2B accounting tool)

**Brief:** LedgerStack homepage rewrite. Segment: Head of Finance / CFO, 2-15 entity scale-up, Series A-C. Awareness level: Problem-Aware to Solution-Aware. Primary unknown: what language do buyers use to describe month-end pain?

**Corpus priority:**
1. G2 reviews for LedgerStack (n=85) and top 3 competitors (n=200+) — problem language and outcome language.
2. Sales call transcripts, last 6 months (n=40) — trigger language and objections.
3. Reddit threads: r/Accounting, r/CFO (search: "multi-entity," "consolidation," "month-end close") — peer language.
4. 8 switch interviews with customers from last 90 days (Moesta protocol).

**Extraction focus:** Month-end pain language; trigger events; objections about setup complexity; outcomes in customers' own words.

**Sample output — copy pattern library extract:**

| Copy function | Customer quote | Source | Frequency |
|---------------|---------------|--------|-----------|
| Problem | "Every month-end is a disaster — I'm stitching files for two weeks" | G2 reviews | 14 mentions |
| Problem | "I can't get a clean consolidated P&L before the board meeting" | Sales calls | 9 mentions |
| Trigger | "We added a third entity and nothing worked anymore" | Switch interviews | 6/8 interviews |
| Trigger | "New CFO came in and said she needed real consolidation, not spreadsheets" | Sales calls | 7 mentions |
| Outcome | "Month-end went from 10 days to 2. I got a week of my life back." | G2 reviews | 11 mentions |
| Objection | "I assumed setup would take months like every other accounting system" | Sales calls | 12 mentions |
| Category | "multi-entity accounting" | Reddit | 22 thread uses |
| Category | "consolidated accounting software" | Reddit/G2 | 18 uses |

**Awareness-level evidence:** The majority of trigger language is Problem-Aware to Solution-Aware. Buyers are searching for "multi-entity accounting software" (Solution-Aware) after a specific trigger event. Homepage hero copy should name the problem (recognition) and immediately offer the solution frame.

**Top Category Entry Points:**
1. Adding the 3rd entity (trigger: complexity threshold crossed).
2. New CFO hire (trigger: new standard-setter joins).
3. Failed audit or Series B diligence (trigger: external scrutiny exposed gaps).

**Company-language / customer-language contrast:**

| Company says | Customer says |
|---|---|
| "Powerful consolidation engine" | "Get a clean P&L without stitching files" |
| "Seamless multi-entity accounting" | "Multi-entity accounting that doesn't take a month to set up" |
| "Real-time financial visibility" | "See the numbers before the board meeting, not after" |
| "AI-powered insights" | "Flags the discrepancies before close" (not used by customers) |

### Example 2 — Switch interview guide (Moesta protocol, marketing version)

**Opening:** "I want to understand the story of how you ended up using LedgerStack — starting well before you first searched. Take me back to about 6 months before you signed up. What was going on?"

**Core timeline questions:**
1. "What was the thing that first made you think, 'this isn't working the way we're doing it'?"
2. "When you first started looking, what did you search for? What words did you use?"
3. "What did you look at before LedgerStack? What made you rule things out?"
4. "What almost stopped you from going with LedgerStack? What were you worried about?"
5. "What happened in the first week of using it that made you think 'this is actually going to work'?"

**Language-mining note:** Record verbatim; do not paraphrase. The goal is not a summary; it is exact quotes. Every time a buyer uses an unexpected phrase, note it with a star — those are the surprises that copy needs.

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1 — Mining without a brief

**Symptom:** The team collects hundreds of reviews and quotes with no specified extraction questions.

**Consequence:** The corpus is large; nothing actionable comes out; the mining exercise takes weeks and produces a document no one reads.

**Fix:** Step 1 is non-negotiable. The mining brief defines what question the corpus is supposed to answer. Mining without a brief is data collection, not research.

### Pitfall 2 — Paraphrasing instead of quoting

**Symptom:** The synthesis says "customers feel overwhelmed by month-end" instead of quoting "every month-end is a disaster — I'm stitching files for two weeks."

**Consequence:** The paraphrase strips the signal. "Stitching files" is a specific, imageable phrase; "overwhelmed" is a generic emotional label. The paraphrase cannot become copy; the quote can.

**Fix:** Collect and output exact quotes. Synthesis annotates them (frequency, source, context) but does not replace them.

### Pitfall 3 — Only mining your own customers

**Symptom:** The corpus is entirely LedgerStack customers; competitor reviews and community threads are skipped.

**Consequence:** The corpus reflects the experience of customers who already chose you. It misses the language of buyers who are still deciding, and it misses the pain that competitor customers experience (which is the pain your positioning must claim to solve).

**Fix:** Include competitor reviews as a primary corpus. The 1-2-star competitor reviews are the highest-signal input for problem language; the 4-5-star competitor reviews are the baseline expectations your copy must meet.

### Pitfall 4 — Treating VOC mining as a PM discovery exercise

**Symptom:** The mining outputs are problem statements, user stories, and feature priorities — not copy patterns, awareness-level signals, or Category Entry Points.

**Consequence:** Marketing has a pile of PM-formatted findings that don't bridge to copy, positioning, or channel decisions.

**Fix:** The extraction questions are different. PM asks "what problem exists and how severe is it?" Marketing asks "in what exact words does the buyer describe that problem?" Both can mine the same corpus; the output format must be specified before mining begins.

### Pitfall 5 — Skipping the company-language contrast

**Symptom:** The synthesis goes straight from raw quotes to a copy pattern library without surfacing the gap between company vocabulary and customer vocabulary.

**Consequence:** The copywriter uses the VOC patterns for body copy but keeps the company language in the headline and subheads. The highest-visible copy surfaces remain in internal vocabulary.

**Fix:** Artifact 4 (company-language / customer-language contrast table) is produced before any copy briefing. The contrast table is given to the copywriter alongside the pattern library.

### Pitfall 6 — One-time mining treated as evergreen

**Symptom:** Mining is done once before a major launch; findings are treated as permanent truth.

**Consequence:** Customer language evolves, especially after category entry points shift (new competitors, new triggers, product maturation). Six-month-old VOC may not reflect current buyers.

**Fix:** Lightweight mining cadence. Before any major copy refresh, channel evaluation, or positioning review — re-check 20-30 recent reviews and 10 recent sales call transcripts. Full mining is a major investment; the lightweight refresh is 2-4 hours and updates the highest-signal patterns.
